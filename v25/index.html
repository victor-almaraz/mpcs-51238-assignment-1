    FORTRAN and the punched card

    FORTRAN, short for formula translation, was created at IBM by a team led by John Backus. Its first compiler shipped in 1957 for the IBM 704, and it let scientists and engineers write their calculations as formulas instead of as machine instructions. The compiler produced code nearly as fast as code written by hand, which persuaded a sceptical profession that a high-level language could be practical. The language spread quickly. FORTRAN II followed in 1958 and FORTRAN IV in 1962, and the 1966 standard, FORTRAN 66, made FORTRAN one of the first programming languages with an official standard. The interpreter in this room runs a subset of that language.

    Programs of that era were not typed onto a screen. A programmer wrote the program in pencil on a coding form ruled in 80 columns, like the one on the desk, and the lines were then punched onto cards, one line to a card, on a keypunch such as the IBM 029, which IBM introduced in 1964 alongside its System/360 computers. As it punched each column, the 029 also printed the character along the top edge of the card, so that a person could read what a machine would read from the holes. A program was a stack of cards held together with a rubber band, and dropping a deck was a real hazard. That is why programmers punched sequence numbers into the last eight columns: a card sorter could put a scrambled deck back in order.

    FORTRAN gives each group of columns on a card its own job, and the coding form shades the groups so that you can see them.

    ColumnsWhat they hold
    1A C or an asterisk in column 1 turns the whole card into a comment.
    1–5An optional statement label: an unsigned number that other statements can refer to. Blanks within the label are ignored.
    6Any character other than a blank or a zero marks the card as a continuation of the statement on the card before.
    7–72The statement itself.
    73–80The compiler ignores these columns, which traditionally held sequence numbers. The interpreter warns you if anything is punched in them.

    A statement too long for one card goes on as many continuation cards as it needs: columns 7–72 of each continuation card are joined to the statement in the order the cards come.

    FORTRAN ignores blanks outside of text, so G O T O 20 means the same thing as GOTO 20. The rule has a famous consequence. FORTRAN has no reserved words, so DO 10 I = 1, 10 begins a loop, but DO 10 I = 1.10, with a full stop where the comma should be, assigns 1.1 to a variable named DO10I. The coding form accepts only the characters that the 029 keypunch could punch, and it turns lowercase letters into capitals, because the keypunch had no lowercase.

    The holes in the card

    The punched card goes back to Herman Hollerith, whose cards and tabulating machines counted the United States census of 1890. IBM’s 80-column card, with its narrow rectangular holes, dates from 1928, and it was still the commonest way into a computer in the 1960s.

    A card has 80 columns and 12 rows. From the top, the rows are called 12, 11, 0 and then 1 to 9. Each column holds one character, and the character is the set of holes punched in that column. The card in hand at the keypunch shows the holes, and prints each character above its column, as the 029 did.

    CharactersHoles
    BlankNo holes at all.
    0–9One hole, in the row of that digit.
    A–IA hole in row 12 and a hole in rows 1 to 9: A is 12-1, I is 12-9.
    J–RA hole in row 11 and a hole in rows 1 to 9: J is 11-1, R is 11-9.
    S–ZA hole in row 0 and a hole in rows 2 to 9: S is 0-2, Z is 0-9.
    SignsMost signs add a hole in row 8. The full stop is 12-8-3, the comma 0-8-3, the left parenthesis 12-8-5, the right parenthesis 11-8-5, the asterisk 11-8-4, the plus sign 12-8-6, the equals sign 8-6 and the apostrophe 8-5. The minus sign is a lone 11, the ampersand a lone 12, and the slash 0-1.

    Rows 12, 11 and 0 are called the zone rows, because a hole in one of them chooses a group of characters, and the digit row then chooses one character within the group; row 0 serves as a digit too. So GO is punched as 12-7 in one column and 11-6 in the next. The letters S to Z start at row 2; 0-1 is the slash. The 029 could punch 64 characters in all. A FORTRAN program needs only the letters, the digits, the blank and the signs = + - * / ( ) , . $ '; the rest serve comments and printed headings.

    The deck

    A job is a single deck of cards. The program comes first and finishes with an END card, and every card after the END card is data for the program to read. On an IBM System/360 the deck would also have needed job-control cards ahead of the program, to tell the operating system who was paying and which compiler to run; here they are not needed. Blank cards at the end of the deck are not sent to the reader.

    Each READ takes the next data card, so the data cards must come in the order the program reads them. If the program tries to read past the last data card, the job stops with the message END OF FILE ON UNIT 5, just as it would have on an IBM machine.

    A data card is read in fields of fixed width, as the program’s FORMAT statement lays them out. The data card of the second sample deck, Heron’s formula, holds the three sides of a triangle under FORMAT (3I5): the first side in columns 1–5, the second in columns 6–10 and the third in columns 11–15. Numbers are best punched at the right of their fields. Blanks before and after a number are ignored, but a blank between digits reads as a zero, so 1 2 in a field reads as 102, as it did on the IBM machines. An all-blank field reads as 0. Under Fw.d, a number punched without a decimal point takes d decimal places from the format, so 314 read with F5.2 is 3.14; a point punched in the field overrides the format.

    Using the room

    The workroom is seen straight on. This manual stands on the bookshelf in three volumes, beside the magazine in its ledge, the Xenakis miscellanea in a file box and the deck box. On the desk below are the coding form, the out tray, the computer and the tape player. Under the desk stand the card reader and line printer, through which every deck that is run must pass, and the UPIC, a tablet on which music is drawn rather than written.

    Taking a thing up
    Whatever can be taken up is outlined in white under the pointer, with a caption beside it that says what pressing it does. Press it to take it up, or choose it from the Menu in the top corner of the page. From the keyboard, the arrow keys move from one thing to the nearest in that direction, Enter takes it up, and H outlines everything in the room that can be taken up.
    Looking
    To look at a thing instead, press it with the right button, or press L with it under the pointer or focused. A line along the foot of the room says what it is. Each print on the wall also offers a deck it calls to mind, to put on the coding form.
    Swapping
    The prints, the plants, the lamp and the mug can be changed: pressing one puts another of its kind in its place. Pressing the bare wall, or Change the wallpaper in the menu, hangs another paper.
    The switch and the light
    The switch on the wall turns the lamp off and on. The light follows the clock of your own computer, so the room is seen by morning, evening or night; Light in the menu sets one of the three instead.
    Sounds
    The room has its own small sounds: the lamp’s hum, the keypunch, the reader and the printer, the tape motor. Room sounds in the menu turns them off and on. Nothing sounds until you first press something in the page.
    Keeping a room
    The room is furnished by chance each time the page is opened. The furnishing is written into the page’s address as it changes, so Copy a link to this room in the menu gives a link that opens the same room for someone else. Nothing is stored.
    Putting a thing down
    Pressing Escape, except while typing on the coding form, puts the object down and returns to the room, and so does Back to the room in the corner. In this manual, the contents list and the Previous page and Next page buttons turn the chapters, and so do the left and right arrow keys.

    The deck box holds every sample deck, each behind a tab with its name, in three divisions: the programs, the music decks, and the decks printed in the magazine, which the magazine’s articles can also put on the form. Taking out a deck puts it on the coding form in place of the cards that were there.

    On the coding form you can also punch your own cards. Pressing Enter adds a new card below the current one, pressing Backspace on an empty card removes it, and the up and down arrow keys move between cards. The Add card, Delete card and Clear deck buttons do the same with the pointer. If you paste several lines at once, each line becomes a card of its own. When the deck is ready, press Run deck.

    Under the form, at the keypunch, lies the card in hand: the card selected on the form, shown with its holes. As you type, the punch head follows the column being punched, and each new column shows its holes at once. The Previous card and Next card buttons step through the deck, and the form’s selection follows along.

    A deck that is run goes first through the card reader and line printer under the desk (see The card reader), and its printout then lands in the out tray. The out tray holds the printer output, the job log with any errors and warnings, a listing of the program, and the output stacker, where the cards that the program punched land. The stacker is emptied at the start of each run. From the stacker you can put the cards on the coding form in place of the deck, add them to the deck as data for another run, copy them or download them as text, thread them onto the tape player, or read them into the computer.

    The computer on the desk has a desktop of its own, with an Editor in place of the coding form, files in place of decks, a Player, a course in punched cards, a calculator, a game, folders of miscellanea, and its own Read Me. The keypunch and the computer share their programs. Type into the computer, under the coding form, opens the deck in the computer’s Editor as text, one line to a card, and Read into the computer, at the stacker, opens the punched cards there. In the computer, Punch on Cards in the File menu sends the Editor’s text back to the coding form, one card to a line. Back to the Room, in the computer’s first menu, or the Back to the room button in the corner, leaves the computer as it is; it keeps its windows and files until the page is reloaded.

    The deck on the form is kept only while the page is open, so reloading the page brings back the first sample.

    The language

    The interpreter understands a single main program; subroutines and functions are not supported. A name has up to six letters and digits and must start with a letter. Unless you declare otherwise, names that begin with the letters I through N hold integers, and all other names hold real numbers. Integers are 32 bits wide, and integer division throws away the remainder, so 7/2 is 3 and -7/2 is −3. Where an integer and a real meet in an expression, the integer is made real. Real numbers have single precision. A variable that has not been given a value holds zero.

    StatementWhat it does
    V = exprThis evaluates the expression and stores the result in V, which can be a variable or an array element, converting it to the type of V. Expressions use + - * / and ** for powers, with parentheses for grouping.
    INTEGER, REALThese declare names as integers or reals, overriding the first-letter rule.
    DIMENSION A(n), B(m,n)This declares arrays of one or two dimensions, numbered from 1.
    GO TO nThis jumps to the statement labelled n. The computed form GO TO (n1,n2,...), I jumps to the Ith label in the list; if I is out of range, it goes on to the next statement.
    IF (e) n1, n2, n3This jumps to n1, n2, or n3 when e is negative, zero, or positive.
    IF (cond) stmtThis runs stmt only when the condition is true. Conditions compare values with .LT. .LE. .EQ. .NE. .GT. .GE. and combine them with .AND. .OR. .NOT.; .TRUE. and .FALSE. are the two logical constants.
    DO n I = m1, m2, m3This repeats the statements through label n while I counts from m1 to m2 in steps of m3, or in steps of 1 if m3 is left out. As on most compilers of the time, the loop always runs at least once, even when m1 is already past m2.
    CONTINUEThis does nothing. It usually marks the end of a DO loop.
    READ (5,f) list or READ f, listThis reads the next data card, laid out as FORMAT statement f describes.
    WRITE (6,f) list or PRINT f, listThis prints a line on the line printer.
    PUNCH f, list or WRITE (7,f) listThis punches a new card into the output stacker.
    FORMAT (...)This describes the layout of a card or a printed line. Iw is an integer w columns wide, Fw.d and Ew.d are real numbers with d digits after the point, nX skips n columns, nHtext and 'text' are literal text, and a slash starts a new line or card. Descriptors can be repeated, as in 3I5, or grouped, as in 2(I3,F6.2).
    STOP, PAUSE, ENDSTOP ends the job, PAUSE writes a note in the job log and carries on, and END marks the last card of the program. A number after STOP or PAUSE, as in STOP 3, is written in the job log.

    The built-in functions are SQRT, ABS, IABS, FLOAT, INT, IFIX, MOD, AMOD, SIN, COS, ATAN, EXP, ALOG, ALOG10, MAX0, MIN0, AMAX1, AMIN1, and SIGN. As the names show, a function that returns an integer begins with I to N, and one that returns a real does not: FLOAT makes an integer real, and IFIX and INT cut a real back to an integer.

    Some notes on FORMAT. A FORMAT statement may stand anywhere in the program, before or after the statements that use it. Printed numbers are set at the right of their fields, and a number too wide for its field prints as a row of asterisks, so 12345 under I3 prints ***. E12.4 prints 12345.0 as 0.1235E+05. If items in the list remain when the format reaches its closing parenthesis, a new line or card begins and the format starts again from its last group in parentheses, or from the beginning if it has none. nH text, the Hollerith field, is named after Herman Hollerith; its count must match the characters that follow exactly, blanks included. The apostrophe form was an IBM extension that later became standard.

    Errors are reported in the job log with the number of the card they occur on: an unknown statement, unbalanced parentheses, an undefined label, a missing FORMAT, a wrong Hollerith count, a DO loop whose last statement cannot be found, a subscript out of range, an integer division by zero, or data that cannot be read. A program that runs a million statements without stopping is halted with TIME LIMIT EXCEEDED, as a computing centre would cut off a job that had used up its time.

    The card reader

    At a computing centre, the programmer did not run a deck. The deck was handed in at a counter, the operators fed it through a card reader with the decks of other users, and the printout came back, often hours later, folded round the cards. Readers were fast: the IBM 2540 card read punch, which served many System/360 installations, read 1,000 cards a minute.

    The card reader and line printer under the desk stand in for all of that. Pressing Run deck on the coding form sends the deck into the reader’s hopper, and the reader takes the cards one by one: the deck in the hopper goes down, the lamp is lit, and each card shows at the read station as it passes, with a count of the cards read so far. Then the printer prints the job’s output a line at a time on its fan-fold paper, with a rule across the paper wherever the program begins a new page.

    The whole deck takes no more than about three seconds to read, and the printout no more than about four to print. Skip to the end of the job finishes both at once; if your computer is set to reduce motion, the job is over as soon as it starts. When the printer stops, the reader says whether the job ran without errors or stopped with errors, and Take the printout to the out tray carries it to the desk, where the job log, the listing and any punched cards wait with it. Go to the coding form returns to the deck instead.

    The line printer

    Results came back on a line printer such as the IBM 1403, which printed a whole line at a time onto continuous fan-fold paper. A chain carrying several copies of the character set spun past a row of hammers, one hammer for each print position, and each hammer fired the moment its character went by. The 1403 printed lines of up to 132 characters, at 600 lines a minute in its early models. The printer here takes lines of up to 132 characters as well; a longer line is cut short, with a warning in the job log.

    The first character of each printed line is never printed. Instead, it tells the printer how far to move the paper before printing.

    First characterWhat the printer does
    BlankMoves the paper one line.
    0Leaves one blank line first.
    -Leaves two blank lines first.
    1Starts a new page.
    +Does not move the paper, and prints over the line before.

    That is why a printing FORMAT usually begins with 1X or 1H , giving the printer a blank to swallow. The rule caused famous mistakes. If a FORMAT happens to put a 1 in the first position, the printer throws a new page, so printing 123 with I3 starts a new page and prints only 23. With I5 the number is printed with two blanks before it, and the first of them is taken as carriage control. Punched cards have no carriage control: on a card, the first character of the record is column 1.

    Iannis Xenakis and the sieve

    Iannis Xenakis (1922–2001) was a Greek-French composer, architect, and engineer, born in Romania to a Greek family. He came to Paris as a political refugee in 1947. He then spent twelve years in Le Corbusier’s studio, where he took charge of designing the Philips Pavilion for the 1958 Brussels World’s Fair, whose curved shells grew out of the same ruled surfaces that the string glissandi of his Metastaseis trace on a graph. He brought an engineer’s habits of mind to music. He composed with probability and set theory, and he was one of the first composers to write music with a computer. In 1962 he ran his Free Stochastic Music program on an IBM 7090 at IBM France in Paris. Xenakis designed the program, and M. F. Génuys and M. J. Barraud coded it in FORTRAN IV. Its output became the ST pieces, among them ST/10 and ST/4, whose full titles record the date the data was generated: 8 February 1962, or 080262. He published the program several times, most completely in his book Formalized Music (1971).

    During a residency in Berlin in 1963–64, Xenakis conceived his theory of sieves, a way of building scales and rhythms out of simple arithmetic. A sieve is made from residual classes. The class written here as m@s (Xenakis wrote m with a subscript s) contains every integer x for which x − s is divisible by m, so 3@0 is the sequence …, 0, 3, 6, 9, …, and 5@2 is …, 2, 7, 12, 17, …. The modulus m sets the spacing and the shift s sets the starting point, so 3@4 is the same class as 3@1.

    Combining classes by union, intersection, and complement produces patterns that repeat exactly but sound irregular. This manual writes the union, the numbers in at least one class, with a bar, as in 3@0 | 4@1. The intersection, the numbers in every class, is written with an ampersand, and the complement, the numbers in no class, with an exclamation mark. Every sieve repeats after the least common multiple of its moduli: 3@0 | 4@1 repeats every 12, and its members in one period are 0, 1, 3, 5, 6 and 9, with the uneven gaps 1, 2, 2, 1 and 3, and then 3 again to reach 12.

    He first used sieves in Akrata (1964–65), for sixteen wind instruments, and in Nomos Alpha (1965–66) for solo cello they select the pitches from a scale of quarter-tones. He set the theory out in “Vers une métamusique” (1967), later a chapter of Formalized Music, and returned to it in an article, “Sieves”, in Perspectives of New Music in 1990.

    The sieve generator deck

    The fifth deck among the programs in the deck box is a sieve generator written in the same FORTRAN that the interpreter runs, in the spirit of Xenakis’s own programs. The first data card after END holds three numbers in 5-column fields. MODE is 1 for the union of the classes, 2 for their intersection, or 3 for the complement of their union. N is the number of classes, from 1 to 10, and LIMIT sets the range, because the program tests every integer from 0 to LIMIT − 1. Each of the next N cards describes one class with a modulus M and a shift S, again in 5-column fields. The shift may be negative: 3@-1 is the same class as 3@2.

    For each integer in the range, the program counts how many of the classes contain it, and the mode decides from the count whether it belongs to the sieve. FORTRAN’s MOD takes the sign of its first argument, so MOD(-1,3) is −1, not 2; the deck therefore computes MOD(MOD(NV-IS(K),IM(K))+IM(K),IM(K)), which is always between 0 and m − 1. Only one flat combination can be made in a run, not a nested one such as (3@0 | 4@1) & !5@2.

    The program prints each member of the sieve along with its distance from the member before it, and then the number of members. It punches each member onto a card of its own, in columns 1–5, so that you can feed the members to another program as data. The sample data describes the sieve 5@2 | 5@3 over the integers 0 to 19:

    CardPunchedMeaning
    1    1    2   20Union of 2 classes, tested on 0 to 19.
    2    5    2The class 5@2.
    3    5    3The class 5@3.

    The sieve contains 2, 3, 7, 8, 12, 13, 17, and 18, and the intervals alternate 1 and 4, because the sieve repeats every 5. Some other data to try: the union of 3@0 and 4@0 below 12 gives 0, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 9; their intersection below 24 gives only 0 and 12, the multiples of 12; and the complement of 3@0 below 7 gives 1, 2, 4 and 5. To see the idea in numbers rather than notes, change the three data cards at the bottom of the deck and run it again. To hear it in notes, thread the punched members onto the tape player.

    Volume 3 · Chapter 10

    The music decks

    The music decks are the second division in the deck box. Each one is a FORTRAN program that punches a card for every note, in the layout that the tape player reads, and each one follows an idea of Xenakis’s. Their data cards come after the END card, and the comment cards at the top of each deck explain what the numbers mean, so you can change them and run the deck again. Most of the decks also print a short account of what they made, such as the scale they built or the first cycle of a rhythm.

    Sieve scale (a melody)
    This deck takes the union of several residual classes over the semitones above a base note. The result is a scale that need not repeat at the octave, and a walk by chance on that scale, up to two steps up or down at a time, makes a melody. The data cards give the number of classes, the range in semitones and the base note, then the classes, then a seed for the chances and the number of notes; a different seed gives a different walk on the same scale.
    Two rhythm sieves
    Two sieves over a cycle of pulses strike a low drum and a high one, and the stroke is louder where they fall together. The data cards set the length of the cycle and the number of cycles, then each drum’s note and its classes. Xenakis built the rhythms of Psappha (1975), for solo percussion, from sieves.
    Stochastic cloud (after Achorripsis)
    Notes arrive at random at a fixed mean rate, in a band of pitch that rises three octaves over the cloud, and the printout sets the counts of notes in each cell of four pulses against the Poisson law. In Achorripsis (1956–57), for 21 instruments, Xenakis used the Poisson law with λ = 0.6 to decide how many events fall in each cell of a matrix of 28 units of time by 7 timbres.
    Markov melody (after Analogique)
    A table of chances decides which of five notes follows each note, which makes the melody a Markov chain. Row i of the table gives, in percent, the chances of going from the ith note to each of the five, so editing a row changes the character of the tune. Xenakis used Markov chains in Analogique A (1958), for nine strings, and Analogique B (1959), for sine tones on tape.
    String glissandi (after Metastaseis)
    Up to eight lines each slide in semitone steps from a note in a cluster to a note in a wide chord, and all the lines take the same time. In bars 309–314 of Metastaseis (1953–54), Xenakis drew the string glissandi as straight lines on a graph of pitch against time.

    The tape player

    The tape player plays the cards in the output stacker as sound. A tape card holds four numbers in 5-column fields, as FORMAT (4I5) punches them.

    ColumnsFieldMeaning
    1–5TIMEThe onset in pulses, counted from 0.
    6–10NOTEA MIDI note number from 0 to 127, where 60 is middle C and each step is a semitone.
    11–15LENThe length in pulses. A blank or a 0 means 1.
    16–20LOUDThe loudness from 1 to 9. A blank or a 0 means 7, and anything above 9 counts as 9.

    So the card     8   67    4    9 plays the G above middle C on the ninth pulse, for one beat, as loud as the player goes. A pulse is a quarter of a beat, so at the starting tempo of 120 beats a minute a pulse lasts an eighth of a second. Notes play in the order of their times, and cards with the same time keep their card order.

    A card with only its first five columns punched, such as a card from the sieve generator, holds a bare number. With the Rhythm switch, the player plays each bare number as a time, so a sieve sounds as a rhythm on middle C. With the Scale switch, it plays the numbers one per pulse in card order, as semitones above the C an octave below middle C, so a sieve sounds as a scale. The switch matters only for bare numbers: the music decks punch full tape cards that set their own times and notes, so for their tapes the switch cannot be moved. A card is skipped if any of its fields is not a whole number, if anything is punched past column 20, if its time is blank or negative, or if it holds more than a time but no note, or a note outside 0 to 127. Cards of text are skipped this way, and the player says how many it skipped.

    Press Thread onto the tape player at the stacker, or Thread tape from the stacker on the tape player, to wind the stacker’s cards onto the reels, and then press Play. Pause holds the place, and Stop rewinds the tape to the start; the counter shows the place in minutes and seconds. The tempo control sets the beats per minute, from 40 to 240. The speed switch changes the tape speed from 7½ to 15 inches per second, two standard speeds of studio recorders, which, as on a real machine, plays the music twice as fast and an octave higher.

    Each note sounds in the wave chosen on the deck’s Wave pad. Tape, the player’s own voice, is a triangle wave with a quiet tone an octave above it; Sine, Triangle, Reed and Saw sound in those shapes alone; and a cycle drawn on the pad with the pointer sounds as it is drawn, as the UPIC’s waves do. A new wave takes effect from the next note. Whatever the wave, the player adds a slow wobble in pitch, the “wow” of a tape that runs not quite evenly, a softened treble, and a faint hiss while the tape runs, as a tape recording would have. Sound starts only when you press Play.

    The UPIC

    The UPIC, the Unité Polyagogique Informatique du CEMAMu, was a system for composing by drawing, devised by Xenakis and completed in 1977 at the CEMAMu, his centre for mathematical and automatic music in Paris. The composer drew on a large tablet linked to a computer. Waveforms and envelopes could be drawn, and then the music itself, with time running across the page and pitch rising up it, so that a line became a sound that changes its pitch as the line climbs or falls. Xenakis made Mycènes Alpha (1978) on it.

    The tablet under the desk is made after the UPIC. On its page, time runs to the right and pitch rises up it over four octaves, from C2 at the foot to C6 at the top, with a darker rule at every C. The faint vertical lines mark the seconds; Length of the page sets how many seconds the page lasts, from 4 to 40.

    Drawing
    Draw on the page with the pointer. Each line is a voice: a level line holds a note, a slanting line is a glissando, a line that branches is an arborescence, and a scatter of dots is a cloud of short notes. A line runs only forward in time, as on the UPIC, so a stroke drawn back to the left is passed over. From the keyboard, the arrow keys move the pen and Space puts it down and lifts it again; the status line names the time and the note under the pen. Take off the last line and Clear the page undo the work.
    The wave
    Every voice sounds in the wave on the small pad: one cycle of it, which you can draw freehand or choose as Sine, Triangle, Reed or Saw. A smooth wave sounds pure, and a wave with corners sounds bright and buzzing.
    Pages to begin from
    Three pages are ready to play or change: an arborescence after Mycènes Alpha, crossing glissandi, and a cloud of points.
    Playing
    Play the page runs a playhead across it, and each line sounds, and is drawn in red, while the playhead crosses it.
    Punching
    Punch the page as tape cards reads each line at every pulse, an eighth of a second at 120 beats a minute, as the nearest semitone, and punches a tape card for each note, with a note held over several pulses on a single card with its length. The cards go into the out tray’s stacker in place of what was there, up to 999 of them, and Thread them on the tape player winds them onto the reels. On tape, a glissando becomes a run of semitone steps, played in whatever wave is chosen on the tape player’s own pad (the UPIC’s wave is not punched with the page).

    From the editors

    This issue is about the early meeting of art and computing, the years when a picture, a poem or a score could come out of a deck of punched cards and a line printer. Its four articles follow pictures made by programs; works left to chance or written as instructions for someone else to carry out; machines that do nothing but follow rules; and rules that loop, wear away and return.

    None of it is only to be read. Wherever an article discusses a deck, a button beside the text puts that deck on the coding form. Run it, and the card reader takes the cards one by one, the line printer answers line by line, and the printout lands in the out tray, often with a few freshly punched cards in the stacker beside it: a picture printed in two characters, a poem drawn from a hat, the tape of a machine that has stopped, or the last generation of a pattern, ready to be fed back in. The catalogue at the back lists all thirteen decks in the order the articles take them up.

    Every deck is written in the same FORTRAN as the rest of the desk. The program comes first; its data cards follow the END card; and the comment cards at the top, the ones with a C in column 1, say what each number on the data cards means. Those numbers are there to be changed. A different seed gives a different poem, a different screen gives a different picture, a different table gives a different machine, and the same seed always gives the same result again, which is the whole difference between chance and noise.

    A word on the decks that use chance. A computer cannot shake a hat, so each one keeps a single number and, at every draw, multiplies it by 171 and keeps the remainder after dividing by 30269. The run of remainders looks random enough to choose words and notes, yet it is completely determined by the first number, the seed, which is why a seed on a data card can be written down, passed on and played back.

    Punch them, alter them, and leave the results in the tray for the next person at the desk.

    01 Computer graphics, 1965–1968

    Pictures from programs

    In 1965 three galleries hung pictures that no hand had drawn. Within three years computer art had a museum show, a front-page scandal and a London exhibition seen by tens of thousands.

    Not one picture was sold.

    Stuttgart, February 1965

    The first exhibition of pictures generated by a digital computer opened on 4 February 1965 in the Studiengalerie of the Technische Hochschule Stuttgart and ran from 5 to 19 February. It showed the work of one man, Georg Nees, a mathematician at Siemens in Erlangen, and it was held under the auspices of the aesthetics colloquium led by the philosopher Max Bense. Nees had helped his data centre acquire a flatbed plotter, the Zuse Graphomat Z64, which drew in ink under the control of punched tape, and he wrote his own libraries in ALGOL to drive it and to supply it with random numbers. For the opening Bense and Nees issued a slim booklet, number 19 of Bense’s series rot, titled Computer-Grafik; Bense’s short text in it, Projekte generativer Ästhetik, is often read as the first manifesto of computer art.

    New York, April 1965

    Two months later, from 6 to 24 April, the Howard Wise Gallery in New York showed Computer-Generated Pictures by Béla Julesz and A. Michael Noll of Bell Telephone Laboratories. Their pictures had been computed on an IBM 7094 and drawn by a General Dynamics SC-4020 microfilm plotter; some were stereo pairs that sprang into depth through glasses handed out at the gallery. The invitation was a deck of four punched cards in different colours. Not one picture was sold.

    Noll had already tested how such pictures were seen. In 1964 he programmed a pseudo-random imitation of Piet Mondrian’s Composition with Lines (1917) and showed copies of both to a hundred people at Bell Labs. Only 28 per cent picked out the Mondrian, and 59 per cent preferred the computer’s version, calling it neater and more varied.

    In November 1965, from the 5th to the 26th, the bookseller Wendelin Niedlich showed Nees together with a young mathematician, Frieder Nake, in his Stuttgart gallery, and Bense opened it. It was the third exhibition of its kind in the world, and all three had happened in a single year.

    A nude in symbols

    At Bell Labs, Kenneth Knowlton and Leon Harmon made the picture that took the idea out of the laboratory. They scanned a photograph of the dancer and choreographer Deborah Hay, reduced it to a grid of grey values, and replaced each cell with one of a small set of symbols, drawn from mathematics and electronics, whose density of ink matched the grey. Up close the print is a field of tiny signs; from across the room it is a reclining figure. Collections date it to 1966 or 1967 and title it Studies in Perception I; it is also known as Computer Nude. The print was about 12 feet wide, and its first public outing was as a prank, pinned up in a colleague’s office. On 10 October 1967 it hung behind the speakers at the press conference in Robert Rauschenberg’s loft that launched Experiments in Art and Technology, and the next day it was in The New York Times. The Museum of Modern Art showed it in The Machine as Seen at the End of the Mechanical Age (25 November 1968 to 9 February 1969).

    The same trick with two marks

    The idea behind Studies in Perception is the halftone, in which a newspaper prints a photograph as dots whose size follows its tone. The deck Studies in perception carries it out with the only two marks this line printer can be asked for: a full stop for paper and an asterisk for ink.

    It builds its picture cell by cell, 64 characters wide and 32 lines deep, and gives every cell a tone from 0 (paper) to 16 (solid ink). The picture is this page’s own: a diagonal wash that darkens toward the lower right, a target of dark and light rings on the left, and a solid black disc on the right. Printer characters are taller than they are wide, so the deck stretches its circles sideways to make them come out round on the paper.

    Then it decides where the ink goes. A data card holds a 4-by-4 screen of sixteen thresholds, the numbers 0 to 15 arranged so that neighbouring cells are as different as possible, the pattern Bryce Bayer of Eastman Kodak described in 1973. Every cell of the picture is compared with the threshold that falls on it as the screen is repeated across the page, and inked wherever its tone is higher. A tone of 1 inks one cell in sixteen, a tone of 8 inks half of them in an even checker, and 16 inks everything. The method is called ordered dithering, and it is how a two-level device fakes a grey.

    What to try: the first data card sets the width of the rings in lines, so a 1 crowds them and a 4 makes a bold target. The second card is the screen itself. Replace it with sixteen thresholds that grow outward from the middle of the square, and the dots clump into a coarse newspaper screen instead of the fine, even Bayer texture. Every row is also punched as 64 digits, 1 for ink and 0 for paper, so the stacker ends up holding the picture as 32 cards of pure data.

    London, 1968

    From 2 August to 20 October 1968, Cybernetic Serendipity at the Institute of Contemporary Arts in London, curated by Jasia Reichardt, set out to show computer-aided creative work of every kind: graphics, music, poetry, dance, sculpture and film, along with painting machines, robots and works in which chance played a large part. It had more than 130 contributors, from composers and engineers to poets, and was seen by some 60,000 visitors. The record issued with it included an excerpt of Iannis Xenakis’s Stratégie (1962), alongside Lejaren Hiller and Leonard Isaacson’s Illiac Suite and John Cage’s Cartridge Music. In three years, pictures from programs had gone from a university seminar room to a public that queued to see them.

    02 Dada, Duchamp and Fluxus

    Chance and instructions

    Long before there were computers, artists handed the making of a work to chance, or to a set of instructions someone else would carry out. A program is both at once.

    “The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

    A poem from a bag

    In 1920 the Dada poet Tristan Tzara included a recipe in his Dada manifesto on feeble love and bitter love. To make a Dadaist poem, take a newspaper and a pair of scissors; choose an article as long as the poem you want; cut it out, then cut out each of its words and put them in a bag; shake gently; take the cuttings out one after another and copy them down conscientiously in the order they left the bag. “The poem will resemble you,” he promised.

    Tzara’s hat follows the recipe to the letter. Its newspaper item is sixteen words long, a small news story from the computing centre itself: THE CARD READER SWALLOWED A DECK OF FORTY HOLES AND THE PRINTER ANSWERED IN CAPITALS NOBODY. The deck shakes the bag by shuffling the sixteen words, then draws them one at a time and prints each on its own line. Now and then, at random, the hand pauses and a stanza ends. Every word drawn is also punched on a card of its own, so the cuttings pile up in the stacker in the order they left the bag, and the run closes with Tzara’s promise, printed in capitals. With the seed on the data card, 191, the poem begins A / OF / FORTY / SWALLOWED; change the seed and the same sixteen words resemble someone else. The second number on the card says how many words to draw, so a poem can stop before the bag is empty.

    Notes from a hat

    Marcel Duchamp tried the same thing with music seven years earlier. On a New Year’s visit to the family home in Rouen in 1913, he and his sisters Yvonne and Magdeleine, both musicians, each drew twenty-five notes out of a hat, from the F below middle C up to high F, and wrote them down in the order they came. The result was Erratum musical, a song for three voices whose parts are headed with their three names, sung to a dictionary’s definition of the verb imprimer, to print. Duchamp later published the score in the Green Box of 1934.

    Erratum musical makes three sets of twenty-five cards, one note on each, numbered as on a modern keyboard from 53 to 77, mixes each set in the hat and draws it out. It prints each voice as a list of draws with the note’s number and name (F3, C#5 and so on), and punches every note drawn as a card holding the voice and the note. Duchamp himself moved some of the highest notes in his own part down to suit a man’s voice; the deck leaves them where the hat put them. The seed on the data card is 1913.

    The canned metre

    In Paris in 1913–14 Duchamp made 3 standard stoppages. His note for it reads: a straight horizontal thread one metre long falls from a height of one metre onto a horizontal plane, twisting as it pleases, and creates a new image of the unit of length. He did it three times, fixed each thread with varnish to a strip of canvas, and had wooden straightedges cut to the three curves. He called the result canned chance, and used the curves again in Network of Stoppages (1914).

    The deck 3 standard stoppages drops its threads as twenty pieces of 5 centimetres, each turned by chance from the direction of the one before, by up to the angle on the data card (0.35 radians, about twenty degrees). It prints the position of the thread every fourth piece, as it lands, and then measures the straight distance from one end to the other: that is the thread’s new metre. With the seed 1914 the three new metres come out at roughly 81, 97 and 96 centimetres, and all three are punched. Raise the largest turn and the threads curl up and the metres shrink; set it to zero and every metre is a hundred centimetres exactly.

    Scores made of sentences

    In the late 1950s and the 1960s, artists around Fluxus wrote works as short instructions for someone else to carry out. George Brecht, who had studied with John Cage, began writing what he called event scores in 1959; Yoko Ono collected more than 150 instruction works in her book Grapefruit (1964). La Monte Young’s Composition 1960 #10, dedicated to Bob Morris, reads in full: “Draw a straight line and follow it.” At the Fluxus festival in Wiesbaden in September 1962, Nam June Paik performed it by dipping his head in ink and drawing the line with his head, hands and necktie down a long sheet of paper on the floor, a performance now known as Zen for Head. In “Paragraphs on Conceptual Art” (Artforum, June 1967), Sol LeWitt described the same handing-over of decisions: “The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

    The oldest instruction piece

    A program is an instruction piece performed by a machine, and Euclid, read aloud performs one of the oldest. In Book VII of the Elements, written about 300 BC, Euclid shows how to find the greatest common measure of two numbers by taking the smaller from the larger, over and over, until they agree. Donald Knuth called it “the granddaddy of all algorithms”, the oldest nontrivial one still in use. The deck uses the remainder, which does a whole run of subtractions at once: replace the pair M, N by N and the remainder of M divided by N, until the remainder is 0.

    What makes the deck a performance is that it reads its own score aloud. As it carries out each card, it prints the card on the left of the page and what happened on the right: M IS 1071, N IS 462; K IS 147; AND AGAIN. With 1071 and 462 on the data card it goes round three times (1071 leaves 147 after 462, 462 leaves 21 after 147, and 21 goes into 147 exactly) and announces the answer, 21, before printing THE PIECE IS OVER.

    It also leaves a second score behind. While it runs, it punches a new program: the steps it actually took, with the two starting numbers written in, every test already decided and the loop written out in full. Load the stacker in place of the deck and run it, and the new program prints THE GREATEST COMMON DIVISOR IS 21 without making a single decision. The first deck is the instruction; the second is one performance of it, written down.

    03 Computation theory

    Machines that follow rules

    A tape, a head and a table of instructions are enough to compute anything that can be computed, and enough to ask questions that nothing can answer. A row or a grid of cells is enough to make patterns nobody has finished understanding.

    Which one leaves the most 1s behind?

    A machine on paper

    In 1936 Alan Turing described a machine that does nothing but read and write symbols on a tape, one square at a time, following a table of instructions. In each state, the table says, for the symbol under the head, what to write, which way to move and which state comes next. Turing argued that such a machine can carry out any calculation that a person following fixed rules can, and that one machine, given the table of another on its tape, can imitate it. His paper “On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem” was received by the London Mathematical Society on 28 May 1936 and read on 12 November 1936. It also proved that some questions about such machines can never be settled by any machine, most famously whether a given one will ever stop.

    The busy beaver game

    In May 1962 Tibor Radó, a mathematician at Ohio State University, turned that limit into something that sounds like a game, in “On Non-Computable Functions” in the Bell System Technical Journal. Consider every machine with a given number of states and two symbols, 0 and 1, that starts on a blank tape and eventually halts. Which one leaves the most 1s behind? Radó called the winners busy beavers, and he proved that no program can find them for every number of states: the numbers grow faster than anything a program can compute.

    Small cases can still be settled by hand and machine together. Shen Lin and Radó settled three states in 1965: the most any three-state machine can leave is six 1s, and no three-state machine that halts runs for more than 21 steps. Allen Brady settled four states in 1983, with 13 ones and 107 steps. Five states took until 2024, when an online collaboration, the Busy Beaver Challenge, proved that the longest a five-state machine can run and still halt is 47,176,870 steps, and checked the proof with the Coq proof assistant.

    A busy beaver runs a three-state champion on a tape of 24 cells, starting in the middle. At every step it prints the step number, the state (A, B or C, and H once it halts), the whole tape, and the count of 1s, with a row of dots underneath that marks where the head is. Watch the head shuttle back and forth laying down 1s; after 14 steps it halts with six of them in a row, and the final tape is punched.

    The machine itself is data. Its table sits on the cards after END, one card per state, and the comment cards give Brady’s four-state champion card by card: put it in, set the first card to four states, start cell 16 and a limit of 200 steps, and watch it run for 107 steps and leave thirteen 1s. Invent a table of your own and the deck will tell you whether it halted, ran off the end of the tape, or was still going when the limit ran out, in which case, it says, it may never halt.

    A row of cells

    A cellular automaton is a row or grid of cells that all change at once, generation after generation, each by looking only at its neighbours. In a 1983 paper in Reviews of Modern Physics, Stephen Wolfram studied the simplest kind, in which each cell is alive or dead and looks at itself and its two neighbours. Those three cells can stand in eight arrangements, and a rule says which of the eight leave a live cell behind, so there are 256 rules; Wolfram numbered them 0 to 255 by reading the eight answers as a binary number. Rule 90 grows a single cell into Sierpiński’s triangle of triangles. Rule 30 grows it into a pattern that looks random, so convincingly that its centre column was later used to make random numbers. Rule 110 was shown by Matthew Cook, in a proof published in 2004, to be capable of universal computation: given the right starting row, it can do anything a Turing machine can.

    Rule 30 runs a row of 63 cells for 31 generations, starting from a single live cell in the middle, and prints one generation to a line with an asterisk for every live cell and a count of the living at the end. Cells beyond the edges are counted as dead. Watch the left edge settle into regular diagonal stripes while the right side churns without ever settling. The last generation is punched in the same form as the row card, so it can replace that card and carry the run on down another sheet of paper.

    What to try: change 30 to 90 on the first data card for the triangles, or to 110 with the single cell moved to column 63, the last, so that the pattern has room to grow leftward.

    Life on a grid

    John Conway’s Game of Life is a two-dimensional relative. It reached the public in Martin Gardner’s “Mathematical Games” column in Scientific American in October 1970. Each cell has eight neighbours. A live cell with two or three live neighbours survives; a dead cell with exactly three comes alive; every other cell is dead in the next generation. Among the first patterns found was the glider, five cells that reassemble themselves one cell further on, diagonally, every four generations. Conway offered a prize of $50 for a pattern that grows without limit, and within weeks a group at MIT led by Bill Gosper had found a glider gun that fires a new glider every 30 generations, for ever.

    Life starts with three of the classic shapes on a grid of 12 rows by 20 columns: a glider in the top left corner, a blinker that flips between a row and a column of three, and a block of four that never changes. It prints every generation with its population, twelve live cells to begin with. The grid’s edges wrap around, so the glider that crawls off the bottom or the right comes back in at the top or the left. After the eight generations on the data card the glider has moved two cells down and two to the right, and the last generation is punched as twelve row cards in the same form as the data, ready to replace them and carry the run on.

    04 Iteration

    Loops and returns

    Do the same thing again, and again. Some rules fall to 1, some fade to silence, some wear a mountain flat, and some come round to where they have been before.

    A tail and a loop, like the Greek letter rho.

    A rule nobody can prove

    Some of the simplest rules are still not understood. Lothar Collatz posed this one in 1937, two years after his doctorate: take a whole number; if it is even, halve it; if it is odd, treble it and add one; repeat. Every number anyone has tried comes down to 1 in the end, and the computers have tried a great many: as of January 2025, every starting number below 2⁷¹ has been checked. But nobody has proved that every number must. The problem has travelled under many names (the 3x+1 problem, the Syracuse problem, and the names of Ulam, Kakutani and Hasse among them), which is itself a record of how many mathematicians it has passed through.

    What makes it hard is that the path of a number is so unruly. 26 reaches 1 in ten steps. 27, its neighbour, takes 111 steps and climbs as high as 9232 before it falls.

    The 3x+1 problem follows a number all the way down, prints each value with a bar of ink beside it, and punches every value on a card. The bars grow with the logarithm of the value, so that each doubling adds the same length and the climb and the fall can both be seen on one page; a number of 9232 and a number of 2 would otherwise not fit on the same sheet. The heading tells you the number of steps and the peak. Try 97, which takes 118 steps and peaks at the same 9232, or 871, which takes 178 steps, the longest journey of any number under a thousand.

    Things that wear away

    The three decks that follow are about things that return a little less each time.

    In Ovid’s Metamorphoses the nymph Echo can only repeat the last words she hears; rejected by Narcissus, she wastes away until only her bones and her voice remain, and her bones are turned to stone. An echo in an empty room calls once, IS ANYONE THERE, and lets the call come back fourteen times, each return quieter than the one before by the ratio on the data card, 0.75. For each return it prints the loudness, the same loudness in decibels (each return is 2.5 decibels down), and a level from 0 to 9, and it punches the level. As the level falls, so does what was said: IS ANY NE THER, then I AN N TH, then only N and H, then a lone N, then a single full stop, then nothing at all from the eleventh return on. Raise the ratio toward 1 for a long, live room; lower it for a dead one.

    In 1969, in the Electronic Music Studio at Brandeis University, Alvin Lucier recorded himself reading a short text that begins “I am sitting in a room different from the one you are in now.” He played the tape back into the room and recorded it again, and again, until the resonant frequencies of the room had reinforced themselves and the words were gone, leaving only their rhythm and the sound of the room. Lucier had a stammer, and the text ends by calling the process a way to smooth out any irregularities his speech might have. The piece was first performed at the Guggenheim Museum in New York in 1970.

    I am sitting in a room plays a row of 40 random levels into a room whose walls blur each point into its neighbours, six reflections to a take, then records it again at full volume, stretched to fill the levels 0 to 9. Each take is printed and punched as 40 digits. Jagged at first, the takes lose their sharp detail first, because the blurring flattens quick changes much faster than slow ones, until all that is left is a single smooth swell, the longest wave the room can hold. The deck stops when one take sounds exactly like the last; with the seed 1969 that happens after take 20, and it prints ONLY THE ROOM IS LEFT.

    Erosion wears a ridge down. Sixty columns of stone, each up to nine levels high, stand in a rough ridge that peaks in the middle. Each year every column may lose a level, by chance; a column that stands higher than a neighbour, or at the end of the ridge, is exposed on that side and wears much faster, so peaks are cut down and edges retreat. Every five years the ridge is printed as a row of heights with the total stone that is left, and punched. With the data card as it stands the ridge starts with 408 levels of stone and after 75 years has only 8 left, a couple of worn stumps; give it more rows, and the deck will report the year in which nothing is left standing. Geologists in the age of William Morris Davis spoke of landscapes as young, mature and old; the printout shows all three on one sheet.

    Things that come back

    The corridor walks from room to room in a building where every room has a single door out, and the door always leads to the same next room: from room r you come to room r² + c, divided by the number of rooms, keeping the remainder. Because there are only so many rooms, the walk must sooner or later come back to one it has seen, and from then on it goes round the same loop for ever. Drawn on paper, the path is a tail leading into a loop, like the Greek letter rho.

    With 997 rooms, c of 7 and a start in room 1, the walk passes through 27 rooms of tail and then a loop of 19, and on the 47th step it finds itself in room 383 again and prints YOU HAVE BEEN HERE BEFORE. That is about as soon as chance would predict. As with the birthday problem, in which 23 people are enough to make a shared birthday more likely than not, a walk among N rooms usually starts repeating after something like the square root of N steps, not after N.

    In 1975 John Pollard turned that observation into a way of factoring large numbers. Walk the corridor with the number to be factored as the number of rooms. Unseen inside it, the same walk is also going round the far smaller corridor belonging to each of its prime factors, and closes its loop there much sooner; comparing the walk with itself at two speeds, the tortoise and the hare, exposes the moment and the factor with it. Richard Brent and Pollard used a refinement of the method in 1980 to split the eighth Fermat number, a number of 78 digits, into its two prime factors.

    Change the number of rooms, the constant or the first room, and every walk is a new building. Every room on the walk is punched, so the stacker holds the whole route.

    13 The catalogue

    Thirteen decks to punch

    Every deck in this issue, in the order the articles discuss them. Each one runs as it stands; its comment cards say what its data cards hold, and what it punches lands in the stacker.

    1. Studies in perception

      A picture 64 characters wide and 32 lines deep, a disc and a target of rings on a diagonal wash, printed in full stops and asterisks by ordered dithering through a 4-by-4 screen. Step back from the printout to see the greys; change the ring width or the screen on the data cards.

    2. Tzara’s hat

      Sixteen words cut from a news item, shaken in a bag and copied out in the order they come, with stanzas broken by chance. Each word drawn is punched, so the cuttings pile up in the stacker; a new seed makes a new poem.

    3. Erratum musical

      Twenty-five notes, from the F below middle C to high F, drawn from a hat once for each of three voices, as Duchamp and his sisters did in Rouen in 1913. Prints each voice by note name and punches every draw.

    4. 3 standard stoppages

      Three threads a metre long, dropped as twenty 5-centimetre pieces that turn by chance, and the new metre that each curve makes. Change the largest turn to curl or straighten them.

    5. Euclid, read aloud

      Finds the greatest common divisor of 1071 and 462, printing each of its own instructions as it carries it out. It also punches a program that gets the same answer without making a single decision: run the stacker to hear it.

    6. A busy beaver

      A three-state Turing machine that halts after 14 steps with six 1s, printing its tape and head at every step. The comment cards give Brady’s four-state champion, which runs for 107 steps.

    7. Rule 30

      A row of 63 cells that grows from a single cell into a pattern that looks random. The last row is punched to carry the run on; try Rule 90 for triangles or Rule 110.

    8. Life

      A glider, a blinker and a block on a grid of 12 by 20 cells whose edges wrap around. Watch the glider crawl one cell diagonally every four generations; the last generation is punched as new data.

    9. The 3x+1 problem

      Halve the even, treble the odd and add one: 27 takes 111 steps and climbs to 9232 before it falls to 1, drawn as a logarithmic bar chart. Try 97 or 871.

    10. An echo in an empty room

      A call returns fourteen times, 2.5 decibels quieter each time, losing letters as it fades into silence. The ratio on the data card sets how live the room is.

    11. I am sitting in a room

      Forty random levels blurred by the room and re-recorded, take after take, until only a single smooth swell, the room itself, is left and one take sounds the same as the last.

    12. Erosion

      A ridge of sixty columns of stone worn down a level at a time, fastest where it stands exposed, printed every five years with the stone that is left.

    13. The corridor

      A walk from room to room by the rule r² + c that must fall into a loop: here 27 rooms of tail and a loop of 19, the shape of Pollard’s rho.

    From the editorial council

    This paper is about Fluxus, the loose and quarrelsome company of composers, poets and artists that George Maciunas named, organised, designed and very nearly paid for, from the festival at Wiesbaden in 1962 to his death in 1978. It came to most people not in galleries but through the post: as cards in a box, as a kit in a briefcase, as a newspaper folded for mailing. Its three articles follow the event score, a whole work in a line or two of print; the boxes, games and newspapers in which Maciunas packed those scores and sold them cheap; and the moment in 1967 when one Fluxus artist handed her lists to a computer and a poem came back on fanfold paper.

    The paper has a model, and does not hide it. In January 1964 Maciunas and George Brecht put out cc V TRE, the first Fluxus newspaper: a single sheet folded to four pages, about 58 centimetres tall, printed by offset. Maciunas laid it out with old clip art, photographs and type of every size; Brecht wrote the editorial. It printed scores, among them György Ligeti’s Poème symphonique, Ben Patterson’s “Man who runs” and Alison Knowles’s “Child Art Piece”; a page of musical texts by Dick Higgins, Emmett Williams and Jackson Mac Low; a photograph of a work by Christo of 1961; texts by Stan VanDerBeek and Eugen Gomringer; and, before long, comic strips, advertisements and order forms. There were eleven issues between January 1964 and March 1979, and the title rarely kept still: cc V TRE, Fluxus cc Valise eTrangle, FLUXUS cc fiVe ThReE. This paper borrows the arrangement and leaves the order forms at the back.

    As in the old paper, the scores are there to be done, not admired. Wherever an article discusses a deck, a button beside the text puts it on the coding form. Run it, and the card reader takes the cards, the line printer answers, and the printout lands in the out tray, often with fresh cards in the stacker beside it: a deck of event cards to hand round, a tape of an open fifth or of metronomes running down for the tape player, a game to be played by ear, or a data card that carries a poem on to its next stanzas.

    Every deck is in the same FORTRAN as the rest of the desk. The program comes first, its data cards follow the END card, and the comment cards at the top, the ones with a C in column 1, say what each number means. The decks that use chance keep a single number, the seed, and at every draw multiply it by 171 and keep the remainder after dividing by 30269. The same seed always gives the same result, so a performance can be written on a postcard and repeated by someone else, which is more or less what an event score is.

    Do it yourself, then leave it in the tray for the next performer.

    01 Event scores, 1959–1964

    Events on cards

    A source of water, an empty vessel, a few words of instruction. From 1959 a handful of artists in New York began to write whole works on cards small enough to carry in a pocket.

    “Second version, Dripping.”

    A drip in a vessel

    In 1958 and 1959 George Brecht, a research chemist at Johnson & Johnson in New Jersey and a painter in his spare time, sat in John Cage’s class in experimental composition at the New School for Social Research in New York. Brecht came away with something smaller and odder: the event score, a work that was nothing but a few printed words, which anyone might perform, or simply read and notice happening around them.

    The best known is Drip Music (Drip Event), written between 1959 and 1962. It reads in full: “For single or multiple performance. A source of dripping water and an empty vessel are arranged so that the water falls into the vessel. Second version, Dripping.” Brecht later remarked that the score draws attention to how beautiful dripping water can be, when most people find a dripping tap merely annoying. At the Festum Fluxorum in Düsseldorf on 2 February 1963, George Maciunas performed it standing on a tall ladder, pouring water slowly into a vessel on the floor.

    Brecht’s events were printed one to a card. In 1963 dozens of them were gathered in Water Yam, a box of loose cards of different sizes published by Fluxus, the kind of boxed edition this paper keeps returning to. A card is the right size for an event: it can be shuffled, dealt, posted or left on a table for someone to find.

    A deck of events

    Event cards makes such a deck by chance. Each card has a title naming a thing in this room (a lamp, a door, the printer, the tape) and one to three lines drawn from a list of twenty, never the same line twice on one card: ON., OFF., FILL., EMPTY., DRIPPING., COUNT THEM., LEAVE THE ROOM. The printer frames each card in a ruled box and numbers it. With the seed on the data card, 1959, the first card is CHAIR EVENT with OFF., LISTEN. and SHUT., and the second is a CARD EVENT with a single line, STOP. None of these is Brecht’s; they are new events for an old desk.

    Every card is also punched, the title and each of its lines on a card of its own, so the stacker ends up holding the deck, ready to be dealt out and performed. The second number on the data card says how many to draw.

    To be held for a long time

    La Monte Young, a composer from Idaho, wrote his Compositions 1960 in that year, most of them as words. Composition 1960 #2 asks for a fire to be built in front of the audience; #5 for a butterfly, or any number of butterflies, to be turned loose in the hall; #10, dedicated to Bob Morris, reads “Draw a straight line and follow it.” Composition 1960 #7 is the exception, and the one that has lasted: two whole notes on a staff, a B and the F sharp above it, and the words “to be held for a long time.” In 1963 Young edited An Anthology, designed by Maciunas and published by Young and Jackson Mac Low, and went on to spend his life on sustained tones. The Well-Tuned Piano, begun in 1964, is played on a piano retuned in just intonation, with every interval a ratio of whole numbers.

    The difference between the two is what you hear in a long fifth. To be held for a long time first does the arithmetic. With A above middle C at 440 hertz, its B sounds at 246.94 and its F sharp at 369.99. The fifth between them is exactly 700 cents, where the simple ratio 3:2 would make it about 701.96. So the third harmonic of the B (740.82 hertz) and the second harmonic of the F sharp (739.99) almost meet but not quite, and the deck finds that they beat 0.84 times a second: a slow pulse inside the drone that a pure fifth would not have.

    Then it plays. Four players hold the two notes, two on each, in bows of three to nine seconds, each a little louder or softer than the last, and since no two players change bow together the sound never stops. Every bow is punched as a tape card for the tape player, 88 of them over 1008 pulses, a little over two minutes at 120 beats a minute. The printout charts each player as a row of digits, the loudness of the bow sounding, with an asterisk beneath every change of bow.

    What to try: put other notes on the players’ cards. A major third, 60 and 64, beats much faster than the fifth; an octave, 59 and 71, does not beat at all. Lengthen the first card’s second number for a longer hold; a multiple of 72 fills the chart exactly.

    A book of instructions

    Yoko Ono, in whose loft at 112 Chambers Street Young had organised a series of concerts from December 1960 to June 1961, published Grapefruit in Tokyo in 1964 with her own Wunternaum Press, in an edition of 500. It holds more than 150 instruction works, nearly all in English and about a third also in Japanese, in five sections: Music, Painting, Event, Poetry and Object.

    Wiesbaden, September 1962

    The scores needed a stage, and Maciunas, then working as a designer for the US Air Force in Wiesbaden, found one. The Fluxus Internationale Festspiele Neuester Musik ran from 1 to 23 September 1962 in the lecture hall of the Städtisches Museum: fourteen concerts on four weekends, with performers from Europe, Japan and the United States. It is usually counted as the founding of Fluxus. Its most notorious performance was a free reading of Philip Corner’s Piano Activities, in which Maciunas, Higgins, Knowles, Patterson, Williams and others took a grand piano to pieces; Corner, who was not there, was shocked when he heard.

    The next year Maciunas issued a manifesto, collaged with photostats of dictionary definitions of the word flux, and copies were thrown into the audience at Düsseldorf. It had three commands in capitals. Purge the world of “dead art”. “PROMOTE A REVOLUTIONARY FLOOD AND TIDE IN ART”. And fuse the cadres of cultural, social and political revolutionaries into a united front. Most of the company ignored the politics and kept the cards.

    02 Fluxus editions, 1963–1966

    Boxes, games and a newspaper

    Maciunas wanted art to be cheap, small, plentiful and funny, and to arrive in the post. He packed it in boxes, briefcases and a newspaper, and he sold it from a shop on Canal Street.

    Arrange the beads such that the word CUAL never appears.

    The newspaper

    Before cc V TRE there was V TRE, a single sheet that Brecht had issued in 1963 for the Yam Festival, its name taken, the story goes, from a neon sign with letters missing. When Maciunas made it the Fluxus newspaper in January 1964, the “cc” marked it as a copy of Brecht’s. The second issue followed in February, the third under the title Fluxus cc Valise eTrangle, and the fourth, FLUXUS cc fiVe ThReE, in June 1964. Maciunas designed every issue but the last two. The newspaper was a programme, a catalogue and an anthology at once: scores to perform, notices of concerts, and lists of what could be ordered from Fluxus by post.

    A hundred metronomes

    The first issue printed a score by György Ligeti, from his brief time in the company of Fluxus. Poème symphonique, of 1962, is for a hundred metronomes, looked after by ten performers with ten metronomes each. All of them are wound, set to different speeds and, after a silence, started as nearly together as possible. Then nobody does anything. The piece ends when the last metronome stops. Its first performance, on 13 September 1963 at the town hall of Hilversum during the Gaudeamus music week, ended in an uproar, and the Dutch television broadcast that had been planned was cancelled.

    Poème symphonique gives one performer’s share: ten metronomes, each given a speed by chance (a tick every 2 to 8 pulses, from 60 to 240 ticks a minute at 120 beats a minute), a winding good for 20 to 35 ticks, and a start up to three pulses late, since no ten hands start at once. The fast ones spend their winding first, so the clatter thins as it goes, and every tick is punched as a tape card on the metronome’s own high note. The printout lists each metronome’s speed, its ticks and when it stops, and then draws them as rows of asterisks that break off one by one into full stops. With the seed 1962 it punches 271 ticks, and the last metronome, ticking 68 times a minute, stops alone at pulse 227, after 28.4 seconds. The second number on the data card sets how many metronomes, up to twenty.

    A box that is a book

    Fluxus 1, the yearbook Maciunas had been planning since 1961, finally appeared in 1964. It was nothing like a magazine. Its pages were manila envelopes and leaves of different papers, holding cards, scores and flattened objects, fastened with three large bolts and mailed in a wooden box. Ay-O contributed a finger envelope with a slit through which the reader felt a nylon stocking; Emmett Williams an opera on a long scroll. It sold for six dollars, or twelve for a Luxus copy with film, tape and objects. Maciunas assembled copies by hand for years, from whatever was to hand, so that no two were quite alike.

    A kit in a briefcase

    The fourth newspaper announced the Fluxkit: a vinyl-covered attaché case with its lid full of newspapers and announcements and its body divided into compartments, each holding a work. A typical kit held a noisemaker by Joe Jones; Mieko Shiomi’s Endless Box, a nest of paper boxes inside paper boxes; Ay-O’s Finger Box, with something to be felt inside; Alison Knowles’s Bean Rolls; film loops, games and score cards. It was portable and cheap by design, a gallery that went to the visitor. In 1965 Maciunas began asking for contributions to Flux Year Box 2, a hinged wooden box with fixed partitions, its compartments filled with small plastic boxes, cards and reels of film, together with a little viewer to watch them through.

    Games without rules

    Games suited the boxes. Brecht’s Games & Puzzles of about 1965 came as small plastic boxes with labels by Maciunas, each with a card of impossible instructions. The Bead Puzzle asks you to arrange the beads so that they are the same, and then so that they are different. The Swim Puzzle reads: “Arrange the beads such that the word CUAL never appears.” His Deck: A Fluxgame is sixty-four cards collaged from encyclopaedia pictures, with no rules at all; the players invent the game.

    Takako Saito made chess. Her Spice Chess, made in the winter of 1964–65 and offered for sale in March 1965, has pieces that all look the same, small corked vials, which are told apart only by the spice inside them. In her Sound Chess of 1965 every piece is the same wooden box and is known only by the noise it makes when shaken: a white knight rattles with a single grain of corn, the black queen jingles with bells. To play, you listen.

    Chess by ear

    Sound chess turns that into a listening game for the tape player. The thirty-two boxes are mixed by chance, and a number of them are shaken one after another on tape. The deck’s own rule, not Saito’s, decides what is inside: a pawn holds one grain, a knight two, a bishop three, a rook four, the queen five and the king six, and the white boxes rattle high and the black ones low. Each grain falls on its own beat of a two-second shake, so the grains can be counted.

    The first page of the printout is a listening sheet, one line per box with a blank to write on; the answers are on the second page. Play the stacker, count, write, and only then turn over. With the seed 1965 the twelve boxes are 28 grains in all, and the first is a black bishop. The second number on the data card sets how many boxes to shake, up to the full set of 32; the third sets the pulses from one box to the next, so a beginner can have longer to think.

    03 Fluxus and the computer, 1961–1970

    A house of dust

    In 1967 Alison Knowles gave a computer four lists, and James Tenney showed her how to make it choose. The poem that came out has been printed, built, moved, rebuilt and recomputed ever since.

    A house of dust, on open ground.

    Four lists

    Alison Knowles had been a Fluxus performer since Wiesbaden; her Proposition #2: Make a Salad, first performed at the Institute of Contemporary Arts in London in October 1962, ended with a salad served to the audience. In 1967 the composer James Tenney ran an informal workshop in FORTRAN for a group of composers and Fluxus artists that included Steve Reich, Nam June Paik, Dick Higgins, Jackson Mac Low, Philip Corner, Max Neuhaus and Knowles. From it came The House of Dust, among the first poems made with a computer.

    Knowles “just made lists”, as she put it. There were four: what a house is made of, where it stands, how it is lit, and who lives in it. Tenney helped her write the program, in FORTRAN IV, that drew one entry from each list, by chance, for every stanza, and the computer printed the stanzas one after another down the paper:

    A HOUSE OF DUST ON OPEN GROUND USING NATURAL LIGHT INHABITED BY FRIENDS AND ENEMIES

    The printout was published, credited to Knowles, Tenney and the Siemens System 4004 that ran it, as green-and-white continuous computer paper folded into a plastic sleeve, by the brothers König of Cologne and New York. A poem built from four short lists turned out to be long: every stanza is a new house, and none of them can be finished.

    A house to live in

    In 1968 a Guggenheim fellowship let Knowles build one of the houses. A quatrain beginning A HOUSE OF PLASTIC / IN A METROPOLIS became a structure in a housing co-operative in Chelsea, New York, where its neighbours did not take to it. When Knowles joined the founding faculty of the California Institute of the Arts, the house went with her and was rebuilt on the campus, now guided by the stanza printed above. Until 1971 it was a place for classes, meetings, readings, meals and performances, among them a sound installation by Max Neuhaus. The poem has kept producing houses since; in 2018 CalArts students built a house of glass from another of its stanzas.

    The deck

    The house of dust draws its stanzas from Knowles’s own lists, shortened to fit a deck of cards: 14 materials, 16 places, 4 lights and 14 kinds of inhabitant, from SAND and BROKEN DISHES to IN A DESERTED AIRPORT, USING CANDLES and PEOPLE WHO LOVE TO READ. That is 12,544 different houses. Each list is a row of FORMAT cards, one per entry, and a draw from the generator picks one card from each row. With the seed on the data card, 1967, the first house is A HOUSE OF STEEL / IN A COLD, WINDY CLIMATE / USING CANDLES / INHABITED BY PEOPLE WHO SLEEP ALMOST ALL THE TIME; with the seed 10763 it is the house that was built at CalArts.

    The run ends by punching one card: a new data card holding the seed it has reached. Put it after END in place of the old one, and the next run prints the stanzas that follow, as though the paper had simply kept running through the printer.

    Tenney at Bell Labs

    Tenney came to the computer before the rest of the company. From September 1961 to March 1964 he worked at Bell Telephone Laboratories in New Jersey, where Max Mathews had written the programs that let an IBM computer compute sound. Tenney was among the first composers to use them, and he used the computer to compose as well as to play. His Analog #1 (Noise Study), finished in December 1961, came from the roar of traffic in the Holland Tunnel; the Stochastic String Quartet followed in February 1963, and Phases, finished that December, was composed by one of his own programs, PLF 5, which set the probabilities and let chance fill in the notes. All the while he was a Fluxus performer and, with Malcolm Goldstein and Philip Corner, a founder of the ensemble Tone Roads.

    Intermedia, and the artist as technician

    Dick Higgins, Knowles’s husband and a fellow veteran of Wiesbaden, founded the Something Else Press in 1963 to publish the books that no one else would, among them Emmett Williams’s An Anthology of Concrete Poetry in 1967. In February 1966 the first Something Else Newsletter carried a single essay, his “Intermedia”, about work that falls between the media, as happenings fall between theatre, music and painting. In 1970 his pamphlet Computers for the Arts urged artists to learn to program rather than hand the work to a technician.

    Paik and the dot

    Nam June Paik had shown prepared pianos and televisions with their pictures pulled out of shape at Galerie Parnass in Wuppertal, from 11 to 20 March 1963. In 1966 he was invited to Bell Labs, where Tenney and A. Michael Noll started him on FORTRAN, and his short film Digital Experiment at Bell Labs shows a single dot leaping about a line on a screen. It is the same joke as Brecht’s drip: the smallest possible event, watched closely, until it is enough.

    From the editors

    Every painting is a long run of decisions: this colour or that one, here or a little lower, one more line or stop. This issue is about painters who gave those decisions away. Some handed them to chance: to slips of paper drawn from a hat, or to the digits of a telephone directory. Some handed them to a system fixed before the first stroke, a proportion or a grid or a sentence of instructions that someone else could carry out. And some, from the middle of the 1960s, handed them to a computer, which is a system that never tires and, given a seed, a chance that can be repeated.

    Our argument is that these were never three separate movements but one long experiment, and that the people who ran it were not trying to get rid of the artist. Ellsworth Kelly let numbers choose his colours, but he chose the colours that went into the hat. Sol LeWitt let draftsmen draw his walls, but he wrote the sentence they followed. Vera Molnár let the machine move the corners of her squares, but she decided how far. In every case the decision that was kept was the one that mattered most: which rule.

    The three features follow that experiment from Paris in 1951 to a laboratory in California in 1973. Each is followed by a few plates, captioned with the title, date, medium, size and collection of the work wherever we could confirm them, and the artists speak for themselves, briefly, wherever their own words survive in print.

    Five of the works are also here to be made again. Wherever an article discusses a deck, a button beside the text puts it on the coding form; run it, and the line printer sets out the picture in characters: a field of squares chosen by a directory, a chart of colours drawn from a hat, lines joined by chance, a wall drawing carried out to the letter, and squares losing their order across a sheet. The printer has no colour and only two marks that make a good picture, a full stop for bare paper and an asterisk for ink, so these are translations rather than reproductions. The comment cards at the top of each deck say what its data cards hold. Change them; a new seed is a new picture, and the same seed is the same picture again.

    01 Colour and chance, 1951–1973

    Colour by chance

    To keep his own taste out of the picture, a young American in Paris numbered his colours and let chance place them. A French toymaker went further, and let the telephone directory paint.

    “Each color had a number.”

    Squares from a stationer’s

    In 1951 Ellsworth Kelly was living in Paris, a young American painter who had come to France in 1948 on the GI Bill and would stay until 1954. He had found, in a stationer’s, papier gommette, the gummed coloured paper that French schoolchildren cut and stick, sold in sheets of flat, even, factory colour. He cut it into small squares and used it for a series of large collages, each on a grid about forty inches wide, which he called Spectrum Colors Arranged by Chance and numbered with roman numerals. In 1953 he made a painting from the sixth, in oil on wood, five feet square.

    The title is exact. Kelly did not choose where any colour went. Recalling the method years later, in a recording for the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, he said: “I had numbered the squares, one through eighteen. I had decided that each color had a number.” Numbered slips were drawn, and each draw said which colour came next in the grid. Chance was a way of keeping his hand, and his preferences, out of the result. What he kept was the palette. The eighteen colours that went into the hat were his choice; only their places were left to luck.

    The deck Spectrum colors arranged by chance repeats the experiment, in the only way a line printer can manage colour: by number. Its hat holds eighteen slips, numbered 01 to 18 for a spectrum that runs from lemon yellow through the reds, purples and blues round to yellow green. For every square of a grid 24 wide, a slip is drawn, read and put back, and the printer sets the grid out as a chart of colour numbers, like the cartoon a sign painter marks up before the paint. A key at the foot names each colour and counts its squares.

    With the seed on the data card, 1951, the first row begins 15 04 10 14, blue green, orange, violet, turquoise. Over 576 squares each colour should turn up about 32 times; in fact lemon yellow is drawn only 23 times and chrome yellow 40, and that unevenness is exactly what chance looks like. Reduce the hat to three colours on the data card and the counts close up. Every row is also punched, so the stacker holds the cartoon card by card, ready to be pasted up.

    The directory as painter

    François Morellet, born in Cholet in 1926, ran the family toy factory there from 1948 until 1975 and painted beside it. From 1958 he made what he called trames, overlapping sets of parallel lines laid down by rules set in advance, and in 1960 he was among the founders, in Paris, of the group that became the Groupe de Recherche d’Art Visuel. He wanted the rule to do all the work, and he wanted the rule to be visible.

    His most famous rule needed a telephone directory. He divided a canvas into a grid of 200 by 200 squares, 40,000 in all, and read the numbers in the directory one digit at a time: an even digit gave a square of one colour, an odd digit a square of the other. He made the picture again and again, in different pairs of colours. Their titles are the method, set out in full: Random Distribution of 40,000 Squares Using the Odd and Even Numbers of a Telephone Directory, 50% Blue, 50% Red is the one the Museum of Modern Art acquired in 2012. The directory is the joke and the point. Nothing could be more orderly than a list of subscribers in alphabetical order, yet their numbers, read as digits, are as good a source of chance as dice.

    Random distribution reads a directory from cards. Each data card is one line of an imaginary directory, a name and ten six-digit numbers, and becomes one row of 60 squares, so that 36 cards make a field 60 squares wide and 36 deep, square on the paper because the printer’s lines are taller than its characters are wide. A key card says, for each digit from 0 to 9, whether it inks the square or leaves it bare. As it stands, the odd digits ink: of 2,160 squares, 1,124 come out as asterisks, a shade over half. Change the key to ink only the zeros and the field thins to 185 marks, one in twelve, a starry sky. The printout ends with the number of times each digit was read, so the directory can be checked against its picture.

    1,024 colours

    Gerhard Richter came to the same idea from the hardware shop. In 1966 he began painting enlarged copies of commercial colour charts, the cards of paint samples handed out to decorators, because they set colours down with no thought of beauty. By 1973 the charts had become a system. For 1024 Farben he mixed his colours from the three primaries and grey by repeated, equal subdivision, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1,024, and then let chance place them. “The arrangement of the colours on the squares was done by a random process,” he wrote for an exhibition catalogue in Brussels the following year, to give “a diffuse, undifferentiated overall effect”. The method still served him a generation later: the window he made for the south transept of Cologne Cathedral, unveiled on 25 August 2007, is 11,263 squares of glass in 72 colours, arranged for the most part by a random number generator.

    02 New Haven, London and New York, 1950–1971

    Painting by system

    A painter may decide everything in advance and still be surprised. Four squares, a bag of numbered cards, a ruled pencil grid and a single sentence each prove it.

    “The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

    Four squares, a thousand times

    In 1950, the year he went to Yale, Josef Albers began a series that he continued until his death in 1976, more than a thousand paintings and prints with one composition. Homage to the Square sets three or four squares one inside the other, each a single colour, and every one sits a little low: the gap below each square is smaller than the gaps at its sides, which are smaller than the gap above. The procedure was as fixed as the format. He painted on the rough side of a Masonite panel primed with at least six coats of white, laid the paint on straight from the tube with a palette knife, generally starting from the centre square and working out, and on the back of almost every panel he wrote down what he had used, down to the maker of each tube.

    With everything else fixed, the colour was free to do the work, and the work it did was to change. “In visual perception a color is almost never seen as it really is,” Albers wrote at the start of Interaction of Color (Yale University Press, 1963), a book that grew out of his teaching. The same grey looks warm on blue and cold on orange; two different colours can be made to look the same. The squares are a laboratory for that, and the system is what makes the experiment fair.

    Chance and order

    In London, Kenneth Martin and Mary Martin had been making constructed abstract art since the end of the 1940s: she made her first reliefs in 1951, and in 1969, the year of her death, she was joint winner of the John Moores Painting Prize, with Richard Hamilton, the first woman to win it. That same year Kenneth Martin began a series of drawings, prints and paintings that he called Chance and Order. The method is simple to state. The crossing points of a grid of squares are numbered; the numbers are written on small cards; the cards are drawn at random, and each pair drawn is joined by a line. In the early drawings each line was drawn as a set of parallels, one stroke for the first line, two for the second, three for the third, always on the same side, so that the order of the drawing can be read in the finished work. Chance chooses where; the system decides how.

    Chance and order numbers the 25 crossings of a grid of four by four squares, shuffles 25 cards, and draws them two at a time. With the seed 1969 the first pairs are 3 and 19, 12 and 18, 13 and 22, 10 and 8, and 9 and 5, and the fifth line, joining the top row to the second, is laid down as five parallel strokes. The printer lists the draw before it draws, so the picture can be checked against its chance.

    The grid

    In New York, the Canadian-born Agnes Martin arrived at the grid by another road. By the early 1960s her hallmark was a square canvas, six feet a side, covered edge to edge with a grid ruled in pencil over thin paint. For The Tree (1964) she worked standing, guiding the pencil with a metal ruler; the lines are exact in plan and slightly unsteady in fact, and that unsteadiness, the hand showing through the rule, is much of what the paintings are about.

    Instructions for someone else

    Sol LeWitt took the hand out altogether. “The idea becomes a machine that makes the art,” he wrote in “Paragraphs on Conceptual Art”, in the June 1967 Artforum, an issue that also printed Michael Fried’s “Art and Objecthood”. In October 1968, for the opening exhibition of the Paula Cooper Gallery in New York, he drew his first work directly on the wall, Wall Drawing 1: Drawing Series II 18 (A & B), in pencil. Soon he was not drawing them himself. What a museum acquires is not a drawing but a document, prepared by the artist, that describes how the work is to be drawn, and others draw it, wherever it is shown. In his “Sentences on Conceptual Art”, first published in the magazine 0–9 in January 1969, he put it plainly: “The artist’s will is secondary to the process he initiates from idea to completion.”

    His vocabulary at first was four kinds of line, vertical, horizontal, diagonal to the right and diagonal to the left, drawn in hard pencil and superimposed. Wall Drawing 11 (1969) reads, in full: “A wall divided horizontally and vertically into four equal parts. Within each part, three of the four kinds of lines are superimposed.”

    The deck Wall drawing 11 is the draftsman. It prints the instruction, then a wall 64 characters wide and 32 lines high in four parts, each part leaving out a different kind of line. The lines are spaced six character widths apart, measured the same way across and down, so that the diagonals come out at true 45 degrees on paper. It ends by checking its own work, a table of which kinds went into which part and then THE INSTRUCTION HAS BEEN CARRIED OUT. Change the data card so that a part holds all four kinds, or only one, and the machine still draws what it is told, but says THIS IS NOT WALL DRAWING 11. IT MAY BE ANOTHER.

    03 Computer art, 1959–1973

    The computer at the easel

    A painter in Paris spent nine years being a machine before she was allowed near one. When the machines came, the painters who used them asked them for the same things: rules, chance, and a little disorder.

    A machine imaginaire.

    The imaginary machine

    Vera Molnár was born in Budapest in 1924, studied at the academy of fine arts there, and moved to Paris in 1947. In 1959, with no computer anywhere within her reach, she began working by what she called a machine imaginaire, an imaginary machine: she set herself a simple program of rules and constraints for combining lines and shapes, worked through its possibilities by hand, and drew the proposals she chose. In 1960 she was among the founders of the group of artists, Morellet among them, that became the Groupe de Recherche d’Art Visuel, though she soon left it.

    In 1968 she gained access to a real computer, at a university computing centre at Orsay, outside Paris, and learned to program it in FORTRAN. The first series it gave her, Interruptions (1968–69), begins with a grid of short straight lines of equal length, turns each by a random angle, and then leaves some out, so that the field of lines is broken by holes. For the rest of her long life (she died on 7 December 2023, a month short of her hundredth birthday) her subject was the meeting of order and disorder, and how little disorder it takes to bring an order to life. She liked to speak of one per cent.

    (Des)Ordres

    (Des)Ordres (1974) is the clearest statement of it. The sheet in the Victoria and Albert Museum is a grid of 20 by 20 cells; in each, squares of several colours are drawn one inside another, and their corners are pushed out of true, more in some cells and less in others, so that the whole field seems to jostle. It was drawn by a plotter, and the paper still has its sprocket holes.

    Order and disorder makes a small sheet of the same kind, four cells by four, with three squares nested in each. Every corner of every square is moved by chance, and the amount grows across the sheet: the cell at the top left is in perfect order, and the cell at the bottom right may move its corners by the full amount on the data card, three character widths as it stands. Set it to 0 and the sixteen cells are identical; set it to 5 and by the far corner the squares have begun to cross one another.

    Stuttgart and Paris

    The computer had reached the gallery almost a decade before Molnár’s (Des)Ordres, in Stuttgart, where Georg Nees and Frieder Nake drew with the Zuse Graphomat Z64 flatbed plotter. Nake, a mathematics student, had written the software that drove it. In 1965 he took as his starting point Paul Klee’s painting Hauptweg und Nebenwege (High Roads and Byroads, 1929), now in the Museum Ludwig in Cologne, and its play of proportion between horizontal and vertical lines. He wrote a program that set up a horizontal and vertical framework and left certain choices within it to the machine, at random, among options he had fixed. The plotter signed it as it drew, Nake/ER56/Z64: the artist, the computer and the plotter. Its title carries a date, 13 September 1965: Hommage à Paul Klee, 13/9/65 Nr. 2.

    Manfred Mohr, born in Pforzheim in 1938, a painter and jazz musician who had settled in Paris, made his first plotter drawings in 1969. From 11 May to 6 June 1971 the ARC at the Musée d’Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris showed Manfred Mohr: Computer Graphics, Une esthétique programmée, the first one-person exhibition in a museum of works calculated entirely by a digital computer and drawn by a plotter, and a Benson plotter drew in front of the visitors. From 1973 Mohr took a single figure, the cube, and made it his alphabet: Cubic Limit draws signs from the twelve edges of a cube.

    A program that draws

    Harold Cohen went further than any of them. He was a British painter of reputation, who had represented Britain at the Venice Biennale in 1966, when he went to the University of California at San Diego in 1968 and learned to program. In 1971 he became a visiting scholar at the Artificial Intelligence Laboratory at Stanford University, and around 1973 he began the program he called AARON. It was not a tool for drawing but an attempt to codify the act of drawing itself, to set down as rules what a person knows when they draw, so that the program could make drawings of its own. AARON at first drew only lines, and Cohen coloured some of its drawings by hand. He went on teaching it for more than forty years, until his death in 2016.

    Every painter in this issue kept one decision back. Kelly kept the palette, Morellet the rule, LeWitt the sentence, Molnár the measure of disorder. Cohen tried to write down the decisions themselves, and found, as each of them had, that the more of the work a rule does, the more depends on the rule.

    From the editors

    Our title is the smallest word in Lisp and the busiest. cons takes one cell from free storage, puts two things in it, and hands back the cell: every list a Lisp program has ever built was built that way, one cell at a time. It was also the name of the first of MIT’s Lisp machines, and its successor was called the CADR, which is a joke only a Lisp programmer would make. This issue is about the language behind the word, from a summer at Dartmouth in 1956 to the Lisp machines of 1980. Looking back in 1979, John McCarthy could already call Lisp “the second oldest programming language in present widespread use”, after FORTRAN.

    The three features follow it in order. The first goes from McCarthy’s notes on the IBM 704 to his paper of April 1960 and the afternoon a programmer turned a mathematical definition into a working interpreter. The second is about rubbish: what happens to a list nobody wants, the manual of 1962 that put the whole language on one page, and the machines built so that Lisp could have a computer of its own. The third is about what the language was for: integrating, simplifying, talking about blocks on a table, and, in the hands of two people at the AI Lab, explaining itself.

    We should own up at once. The FORTRAN on this desk is close kin to the language Lisp was invented to get away from. It has no recursion. Its card reader reads only numbers, and its printer cannot put a parenthesis anywhere it was not told to put one in advance. McCarthy knew the problem: in the late 1950s, he wrote, “keypunches and printers with adequate character sets didn’t exist”. So the five decks in this issue do in FORTRAN what the articles describe, and show what it costs. A list is two arrays and a free-storage list. Recursion is a push-down list kept by hand.     01 Dartmouth, IBM and MIT, 1956–1962

    Contents of the address part

    John McCarthy wanted to write programs that reason, in a language as algebraic as FORTRAN. FORTRAN could not be made to do it, so he wrote a paper instead, and a programmer who had been told the paper was not meant to be run ran it.

    “This eval is intended for reading, not for computing.”

    A word in four parts

    The idea came to John McCarthy in the summer of 1956, at the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, the first organised study of the subject. There Allen Newell, Cliff Shaw and Herbert Simon described IPL 2, the list-processing language in which they had written their Logic Theorist for the RAND Corporation’s JOHNNIAC. McCarthy liked the lists but wanted them written algebraically, as FORTRAN wrote arithmetic, so that the part of a part of an expression could be taken by composing two functions.

    The machine was to be the IBM 704, then on its way to a new computation centre at MIT. Its word was 36 bits, and two 15-bit parts of it, the address and the decrement, had instructions of their own; 15 bits was also the size of an address, so a list could be built of words that pointed at words. So car came to mean “Contents of the Address part of Register number” and cdr the same for the decrement; cpr and ctr, for the word’s two small remaining parts, did not survive. A way to take a word off the free-storage list and fill it was needed too. All this was done at Dartmouth, on paper: the 704 had not yet arrived.

    Car and cdr keeps the 704’s word in two arrays, ICAR and ICDR, forty cells of each, with a CDR of 0 for NIL, the end of a list, and every unused cell chained on a free-storage list. It conses the five digits on its data card into a list, reverses the list and appends the reverse to the original, printing every cons: the cell it took and where the free list now begins. With 3 1 4 1 5 on the card, the printer gives (3 1 4 1 5) at cell 5, (5 1 4 1 3) at cell 10 and the appended list of ten atoms at cell 15, using fifteen cells where twenty might be expected. The deck then follows the appended list and finds that after five new cells it runs straight into cell 10: append copied its first list and simply pointed at the second, a sharing that is the whole economy of Lisp.

    A list language inside FORTRAN

    IBM had its own reason to want lists: a program to prove theorems in plane geometry, on an idea of Marvin Minsky’s, for which McCarthy was a consultant. Nathaniel Rochester and Herbert Gelernter decided, on McCarthy’s advice, to build list processing inside FORTRAN, since a compiler for a new language was then thought to take many man-years. Gelernter and Carl Gerberich made it FLPL, the FORTRAN List Processing Language, and noticed something McCarthy had missed: cons should be a function whose value is the cell it fills, so that new expressions could be built by nesting conses. FLPL served the geometry program well. But it had, in McCarthy’s words, “neither conditional expressions nor recursion”, and every list had to be erased by hand.

    McCarthy had already felt the lack. Writing chess legal-move routines in FORTRAN at MIT in 1957–58, he had invented a function XIF(M,N1,N2) whose value was N1 or N2 as M was zero or not. FORTRAN computed both branches before the call, which led him to the true conditional expression, which evaluates only one. In the summer of 1958, at IBM, he chose differentiating algebraic expressions as a sample problem. Differentiation is recursive by nature, and writing it down needed recursive definitions, conditional expressions and, to pass functions as arguments, the lambda notation of Alonzo Church’s book of 1941. “I didn’t understand the rest of his book,” McCarthy admitted, so he was not tempted to implement more of it. FLPL could not express the result, and it was never run that summer.

    A room and a keypunch

    In the autumn of 1958 McCarthy became an assistant professor at MIT, and he and Minsky began the MIT Artificial Intelligence Project. There was no written proposal. Asked what they needed, they asked for “a room, two programmers, a secretary and a keypunch”. The first functions were compiled into assembly language by hand, and recursion was managed by SAVE and UNSAVE routines that kept variables and return addresses on a single public stack. Programs were drafted in M-expressions, a FORTRAN-like notation with square brackets, which the IBM 026 keypunch could not even punch; data were S-expressions, so that x + 3y + z became (PLUS X (TIMES 3 Y) Z), a notation later nicknamed “Cambridge Polish”.

    The tower of Hanoi shows what that stack is for. Édouard Lucas sold the puzzle in 1883 under the name N. Claus de Siam, an anagram of Lucas d’Amiens, and its solution is the classic recursion: move all but the largest disc out of the way, move the largest, then move the rest back on top. The deck cannot call itself, so every call not yet finished is a frame on a push-down list, and each move is printed with the list drawn beside it, an asterisk to a frame, and the three pegs read from the bottom up. Four discs take fifteen moves, as 2⁴ - 1 says they must, with 31 frames pushed and never more than five waiting at once; seven discs take 127 moves and a list eight deep.

    A universal function

    McCarthy wanted to show that his language was a neater way of describing computable functions than Turing machines, and the proof was a universal function, eval, that computes the value of any Lisp expression given as a Lisp list. It appeared in “Recursive Functions of Symbolic Expressions and Their Computation by Machine, Part I”, in Communications of the ACM for April 1960. Part II was never written. S. R. Russell, known as Steve, saw that eval could serve as an interpreter. McCarthy remembered his own reply: “ho, ho, you’re confusing theory with practice, this eval is intended for reading, not for computing.” Russell hand-coded it for the 704 anyway, and Lisp had an interpreter. The unexpected appearance of one, McCarthy wrote, “tended to freeze the form of the language”. The parentheses stayed. Russell went on to begin Spacewar! on the PDP-1.

    02 From the 704 to the Lisp machine, 1960–1980

    Garbage collection

    Lisp never asks its programmer to throw anything away. Something has to, and for twenty years the answer was a few seconds of marking and sweeping, a page of definitions, and at last a computer built around the problem.

    “We already called this process ‘garbage collection’.”

    Reclamation

    Since car and cdr copy nothing, lists share their parts, and a cell may be wanted by several lists or by none. IPL erased lists explicitly, which McCarthy found “clearly unaesthetic”, and counting references would have needed room the 704’s word did not have. So storage would be abandoned until the free-storage list ran out; then everything still reachable would be found, and the rest gathered up.

    The paper of 1960 describes it plainly. About 15,000 registers start on the free-storage list. When none is left, a “reclamation cycle” begins: starting from a fixed set of base registers, every register that can be reached by a chain of cars and cdrs has its sign made negative; then a sweep through memory puts every register whose sign did not change back on the free list, and turns the others positive again. It took several seconds, so it had better recover several thousand registers. In a footnote to a later reprint of the paper, McCarthy confessed: “We already called this process ‘garbage collection’, but I guess I chickened out of using it in the paper.”

    Reclamation runs the same cycle on sixty cells. It builds lists at random, from a seed, and puts each in one of four base registers, abandoning whatever was there; now and then an element is not an atom but another register’s list, so structure is shared. When the cells run out, it marks from the four registers and from the list half built (the protection Lisp 1.5 gave its partial results), keeping the cells still to visit on a push-down list, and then sweeps. At each collection the printer draws storage under a ruler, an asterisk for a cell still in use and a full stop for one reclaimed. With seed 1960, eighty lists take 229 conses from sixty cells and four collections; the first finds twelve cells in use and returns 48. Raise the chance of sharing to five in ten and the shared lists hold on to more and more: the fifth collection finds 38 cells in use, and with 99 lists instead of 80 the seventh finds all sixty reachable, and the deck reports that nothing could be reclaimed.

    Page 13

    The LISP 1.5 Programmer’s Manual was published by the MIT Press in 1962. Its preface, dated 17 August 1962, says that the manual was written by Michael Levin, that the interpreter was programmed by Stephen Russell and Daniel Edwards, that Edwards also wrote the garbage collector and the arithmetic, and that the compiler was the work of Timothy Hart and Levin. At the foot of page 13 are the definitions of evalquote, apply, eval, evcon and evlis: the whole interpreter, a few lines long. Alan Kay read them as a graduate student and called them “Maxwell’s Equations of Software!”

    The manual is also a document of the card age. “There is no particular card format for writing LISP,” it says: columns 1 to 72 of any number of cards, card boundaries ignored. A run was a series of packets, each ending with the word STOP, which should be followed “by a large number of right parentheses” in case the count had gone wrong, and the deck finished with two blank cards to keep the reader from hanging up. A garbage collection on the 7090 took about a second, and could be recognised by the stationary pattern of the MQ lights.

    Eval is our page 13, at more length because it is in FORTRAN. Each token of an expression is a data card: a code in column 1, a number or the name of a function in the next nine columns, and the token as written further along, for people. A reader with a push-down list builds the expression as cells; eval then works through it with a frame for every function not yet finished, and prints a trace as each one begins and ends. It knows four functions of Lisp 1.5, PLUS and TIMES of any number of arguments and DIFFERENCE and QUOTIENT of exactly two. (PLUS 1 (TIMES 3 4) 5), McCarthy’s example with numbers for letters, gives 18; a longer expression three lists deep gives 42. The last card says STOP.

    Wizards down the hall

    By 1964 Lisp 1.5 ran on the IBM 7094 under MIT’s Compatible Time-Sharing System. That spring DEC and members of MIT’s Tech Model Railroad Club wrote a Lisp for the new PDP-6, the first program written on the machine and the ancestor of MacLisp. On the PDP-6 and the PDP-10 after it, a 36-bit word with 18-bit addresses held a whole cons cell, and half-word instructions reached the car and the cdr. At Bolt Beranek and Newman another line grew into BBN Lisp, renamed Interlisp in 1973 when Xerox PARC shared its upkeep. Interlisp had DWIM, “Do What I Mean”, which corrected spelling and could even see that an 8 was a left parenthesis typed without the shift key on a Model 33 teletype. Guy Steele and Richard Gabriel later summed up the period as “an AI lab with a Lisp wizard down the hall”.

    A machine of its own

    In 1974 Richard Greenblatt started the MIT Lisp Machine project with Thomas Knight, Jack Holloway and Pitts Jarvis. Greenblatt’s working paper, The LISP Machine, and Knight’s CONS appeared that November. The aims were a single-user machine at less than $70,000, hardware support for type checking and garbage collection, and large bit-mapped displays. The CONS was built, then an improved design, the CADR, of which some dozens were made; in 1979 and 1980 two companies, Lisp Machines Incorporated and Symbolics, set out to sell them. There is a last irony. For some years the early Lisp machines had no garbage collector in use, and users preferred to leave it off: their memories were large enough to work for days or weeks before saving the whole “world” to disk and starting again.

    03 Project MAC and the AI Lab, 1961–1978

    Symbols at work

    A language for symbols was built to do things with them: integrate, simplify, converse. The longest-lived thing it did was to describe itself, and to show that a procedure call need be no dearer than a GO TO.

    “Pick up a big red block.”

    Algebra by machine

    McCarthy’s unwritten Part II was to have shown “applications to computing with algebraic expressions”, and his students wrote it instead. James Slagle’s doctoral thesis of 1961, supervised by Minsky, described SAINT, the Symbolic Automatic INTegrator, written in Lisp and run interpretively on an IBM 7090. Set 54 problems from MIT’s freshman calculus examinations, it solved 52, taking about two minutes each.

    Project MAC opened at MIT on 1 July 1963, with a two-million-dollar grant from ARPA and Robert Fano as director, and the AI group worked within it until it became a laboratory of its own in 1970. In July 1968 Carl Engelman, William Martin and Joel Moses began Macsyma, Project MAC’s SYmbolic MAnipulator. It was written in MacLisp, and was perhaps the largest Lisp program of its day, large enough to change the language: arbitrary-precision integers were added to MacLisp in 1970 or 1971 because Macsyma’s users needed them.

    Two conversations

    Not every famous program of the AI Lab’s neighbourhood was written in Lisp. Joseph Weizenbaum’s ELIZA, described in 1966, was written in MAD-SLIP for the IBM 7094 under CTSS. But Bernie Cosell soon wrote a Lisp ELIZA at Bolt Beranek and Newman, and when BBN became one of the first sites on the ARPANET, that Lisp ELIZA travelled across it and became the strain most people knew.

    SHRDLU was Lisp from the start. Terry Winograd wrote it at MIT between 1968 and 1970, in Lisp and Micro-Planner on a PDP-6 with a graphics terminal, and named it after ETAOIN SHRDLU, the order of the keys on a Linotype machine. Its world was a table of blocks and pyramids, its conversation began “Pick up a big red block”, and when told to grasp the pyramid it replied that it did not understand which pyramid was meant. The thesis that describes it, published as an AI Lab report in February 1971, is called Procedures as a Representation for Data, which is as good a short description of Lisp as any.

    The ultimate

    In the autumn of 1975 Gerald Jay Sussman and Guy Steele set out to understand Carl Hewitt’s theory of actors by building a toy. They wrote a tiny Lisp interpreter in MacLisp, lexically scoped because Sussman had just been studying Algol, and added actors to it. When it worked, they found that the code in apply for calling a function and for sending a message to an actor was identical: actors and closures were the same thing. They called the interpreter Schemer, after Planner and Conniver, but the ITS operating system allowed file names of only six characters, and Scheme it became. This room’s FORTRAN has the same rule for the names of its variables.

    The report, AI Memo 349 of December 1975, was the first of the Lambda Papers. Lambda: The Ultimate Imperative and Lambda: The Ultimate Declarative followed in 1976, and in October 1977 Steele’s AI Memo 443 set out to debunk the “expensive procedure call” myth, under the third of its titles, Lambda: The Ultimate GOTO. A call that is the last thing a procedure does needs nothing kept for it; it can be compiled as a jump that carries its arguments with it.

    Ackermann’s function puts that to work in FORTRAN. Wilhelm Ackermann gave his function in 1928 as one that can be computed but is not primitive recursive, so that no program made only of DO loops, however nested, can compute it; the deck uses the two-argument form of Rózsa Péter and Raphael Robinson. Of its three cases, two end in a tail call, and the deck does those with a GO TO; only the inner call of A(m, A(m, n-1)) leaves an outer call waiting, and only that goes on the push-down list. It traces A(2,1), five in fourteen steps, then tables the function up to A(3,5), which is 253, found in 42,438 steps with 251 calls waiting at the deepest.

    Sussman and Steele went on: a revised report in 1978, a compiler called RABBIT, and The Art of the Interpreter, a set of small Lisp interpreters with variations, titled after The Art of the Fugue. It was rejected by an ACM journal. The idea it served, that a language is best understood by writing its interpreter in itself, had been on page 13 since 1962.

    Five decks to punch

    Every deck in this issue, in the order the articles discuss them. Each one runs as it stands; its comment cards say what its data cards hold. None of them calls itself: where Lisp would recurse, each keeps a push-down list by hand.

    1. Car and cdr

    From the editors

    Before there is any silver, any developer or any print, there is a hole in a box and the light that comes through it. Everything a photograph will ever be is decided in the few centimetres between the front of the camera and the film: how big the picture is, how much of the world it takes in, how bright it is, which parts are sharp and which are not. That is optics, and it is the oldest part of photography by eight hundred years.

    This issue is about the arithmetic of those few centimetres. A scholar in Cairo who watched candles through a hole in a wall and saw that light runs in straight lines. A Venetian who put a spectacle lens in the hole, and then covered most of it to make the picture sharper. A mathematician in Vienna who computed a lens with the help of a squad of gunners and made portraits twenty times quicker. A peer of the realm who worked out how big a pinhole should be. A group of Californians who named themselves after the smallest stop on their lenses. And the lens makers of Paris, Vienna, York and Jena who, one fault at a time, taught glass to draw a straight line to the corner of the frame.

    Our argument is that the photographers who mattered knew this arithmetic, whether or not they ever wrote it down. Every formula in these pages has a worked example in real numbers, with millimetres a reader can measure on their own camera, and every one of them was once a rule of thumb that someone had to discover.

    Five of them are here to be run. Wherever an article discusses a deck, a button beside the text puts it on the coding form. The decks find the best pinhole for a camera of any length, follow a lens through its focusing and draw its rays, set out every stop against every shutter speed, chart the depth of field of a lens across its stops, and trace rays through a single lens to show where they go wrong. The line printer draws with a full stop for paper and an asterisk for ink, which is exactly what a ray diagram needs. The comment cards at the top of each deck say what its data cards hold; change them, and the optics change with them.

    01 The camera obscura and the pinhole, 1000–1891

    The dark chamber

    Make a small hole in the wall of a dark room and the street appears upside down on the opposite wall. It took eight centuries to explain why, and to work out how small the hole should be.

    A camera with only a pin-hole.

    Candles and a window

    In the early eleventh century the scholar Ibn al-Haytham, known in Latin Europe as Alhazen, set several candles in a row before an opening into a dark recess. On the white wall inside, each candle made its own spot of light, directly opposite it on a straight line through the opening; shield one candle and only its spot went out. The lights did not mingle in the air. That experiment, in his Book of Optics, is the principle of every camera. Each point of the scene sends its light through the hole along a straight line, and so lands at one place on the wall, above for what is below and left for what is right. In a treatise on the shape of the eclipse he noted that a partly eclipsed sun shows as a crescent on the wall only when the hole is very small.

    The Frisian-born physician and mathematician Gemma Frisius watched the solar eclipse of 24 January 1544 this way at Louvain, and his illustration of the next year, in De radio astronomico et geometrico, is the earliest known picture of a camera obscura. The name, the dark chamber, is usually credited to Johannes Kepler, in 1604.

    The geometry is that of similar triangles. If the hole is a distance f from the wall, a subject of height h at distance u makes an image of height

    h' = h × f/u

    A tree 10 metres tall and 20 metres away, through a box 100 mm deep, makes an image 10,000 × 100/20,000 = 50 mm tall, upside down. That depth is the pinhole camera’s focal length.

    How small a hole

    A hole focuses nothing. Every point of the scene throws a patch of light about the size of the hole, so a hole of 1 mm draws every point as a disc 1 mm across. Make the hole smaller and the patch shrinks, but only so far, because light through a small opening spreads by diffraction, and the smaller the opening the more it spreads: through a hole of 0.1 mm in the same box, green light spreads into a disc about 2.44 × 0.00055 × 100/0.1 = 1.3 mm across. Between the two lies a best hole.

    Joseph Petzval, of whom more in our third feature, was the first to publish a rule for it, in 1857. For a box of depth f and light of wavelength λ, his best diameter was

    d = sqrt(2fλ)

    Lord Rayleigh came back to the question in 1891, in “On pin-hole photography” in the Philosophical Magazine, and was the first to treat it by the wave theory of light. His conclusion, in Nature the same year, was that the hole “may advantageously be enlarged beyond that given by Petzval’s rule”: he suggested a radius of sqrt(fλ), that is, a diameter of

    d = 2 × sqrt(fλ)

    Take the 100 mm box and green light of 550 nanometres, 0.00055 mm. Then fλ = 0.055 mm², its square root is 0.2345 mm, and Rayleigh’s hole is 0.469 mm across; Petzval’s is 0.332 mm. A box four times as long needs a hole only twice as wide.

    The price is light. The f-number of the pinhole, depth over diameter, is 100/0.469 = 213, and a lens at f/16 lets in (213/16)² = 177 times as much, seven and a half stops more. Where the rule of thumb of our next feature gives 1/100 second at f/16, the pinhole needs 177/100 = 1.8 seconds, and in practice more, because film loses speed in dim light.

    The best pinhole does the sums for eight boxes from 25 to 500 mm deep: both rules, the f-number, the stops beyond f/16 and the exposure. The 25 mm box takes a hole of 0.235 mm at f/107 and four-tenths of a second, the 500 mm box a hole of 1.049 mm at f/477 and nearly nine seconds.

    The first known description of pinhole photography is in David Brewster’s The Stereoscope of 1856: “a camera without lenses, and with only a pin-hole”. In 1890 George Davison won a medal at the Photographic Society’s exhibition with An Old Farmstead, taken through a pinhole, its softness the point; Alfred Stieglitz printed it in Camera Work in 1907 as The Onion Field.

    Glass in the hole

    A lens is a hole that can be large without blurring, because it bends every ray from one point of the subject back to one point of the image. In 1568 the Venetian Daniele Barbaro, in La pratica della perspettiva, described a camera with a lens from an old man’s spectacles, and advised covering the lens until only a little of the middle was left open, because the picture was then sharper: the first diaphragm. William Henry Fox Talbot’s small cameras of 1835, known as mousetraps, had lenses, and with one of them in August of that year he made the oldest surviving negative, a window at Lacock Abbey.

    For a simple lens of focal length f, a subject at distance u in front comes to focus at distance v behind, by the thin-lens equation:

    1/f = 1/u + 1/v

    and the image is inverted and magnified by

    m = v/u

    Focus a 50 mm lens on a person 3 metres away: 1/v = 1/50 - 1/3,000 = 59/3,000, so v = 50.85 mm. The lens moves forward 0.85 mm, and the magnification is 50.85/3,000 = 0.0169, one fifty-ninth: a person 1.75 m tall is 29.7 mm high on the film and fits the long side of a 24 × 36 frame. At 2 metres, at 44.9 mm, they do not. At u = 2f = 100 mm, v is 100 mm too, the image is life size, and the light that formed a small image is spread over a large one: the exposure has to grow by (1 + m)², four times, two stops.

    The thin lens sets out those figures at eight distances, from 40 mm (inside the focal length, where there is no real image) to 10 metres. Then it draws the three textbook rays for a subject 150 mm away: one parallel to the axis and bent through the focal point behind; one straight through the centre; one through the focal point in front, leaving parallel. All three meet 75 mm behind the lens at the tip of an image half the size.

    02 Aperture, exposure and depth, 1835–1932

    Stopping down

    Every lens has a ring of numbers that goes up by the square root of two. Turn it one way and the picture is brighter; the other way and more of it is sharp, until the light itself begins to blur.

    US 256, or f/64.

    The number on the ring

    The brightness of the image a lens makes depends on two lengths: the diameter D of its opening, which sets how much light it gathers, and its focal length f, which sets how large an image it spreads that light across. Their ratio is the f-number:

    N = f/D

    A 50 mm lens with an opening 25 mm across is at f/2; close the diaphragm to 6.25 mm and it is at f/8. The light gathered grows with the area of the opening, and the area of the image with the square of the focal length, so the brightness of the image goes as 1/N². Any lens at f/8 gives the same exposure as any other lens at f/8, whatever its focal length, and that is the whole use of the number.

    To halve the light, halve the area, which means dividing the diameter by the square root of two, 1.414. So the stops go up by that factor, each one letting in half the light of the one before: 1, 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22, 32, 45, 64. Every second stop is a power of two. The others are rounded: f/11 is really 11.31, f/22 is 22.63 and f/45 is 45.25.

    The notation took time to settle. In the 1880s the Photographic Society of Great Britain adopted the Uniform System, in which US 16 was f/16 but the numbers followed the exposure, not the diameter: f/11 was US 8, f/8 was US 4, f/32 was US 64. The rule is US = N²/16, and Kodak was still marking its cameras that way in the 1920s.

    Exposure by number

    What reaches the film is the brightness of the image times the time the shutter is open, t. Both are folded into a single figure, the exposure value:

    EV = log2(N²/t)

    At f/16 and 1/125 second, N²/t = 256 × 125 = 32,000, and log2 of 32,000 is 14.97, so EV 15. At f/8 and 1/500, N²/t = 64 × 500 = 32,000 again: two stops wider, two steps of the shutter shorter, the same exposure. That is the old rule of thumb, sunny sixteen: in bright sun, f/16 at one over the film speed.

    Sunny sixteen turns the rule into exposure values for any film speed and light. As it stands, for ASA 100 in bright sun, it works out EV 14.64, calls it 15, prints every stop from f/1.4 to f/32 against every speed from one second to 1/1000, and lists the six settings that give the rule’s exposure.

    A lamp or a flash, unlike the sun, is near, and its light falls off with the square of the distance:

    E = I/r²

    Twice the distance is a quarter of the light, two stops. Flash guns are rated by a guide number, GN = N × r, the stop times the distance. With a guide number of 32 in metres, a subject 4 metres away needs f/8; at 8 metres, f/4, two stops wider for twice the distance.

    How much is sharp

    A lens focused at one distance brings only that distance to a point. Nearer and farther points come to a focus in front of or behind the film and draw small discs instead. If the discs are small enough they pass for points, and the limit is the circle of confusion, c, commonly taken as 0.03 mm for the 24 × 36 frame, about a fourteen-hundredth of its diagonal. A smaller stop makes narrower cones of light, so smaller discs, and a deeper zone that passes for sharp.

    The key distance is the hyperfocal distance:

    H = f²/(Nc) + f

    For a 50 mm lens at f/8, H = 2,500/(8 × 0.03) + 50 = 10,467 mm. Focus at 10.5 metres and everything from half that, 5.2 metres, to the horizon is sharp. Focused at a distance s, the near and far limits are

    near = s(H - f)/(H + s - 2f)

    far = s(H - f)/(H - s)

    At f/8 and 3 metres that is 3,000 × 10,417/13,367 = 2.34 m to 3,000 × 10,417/7,467 = 4.19 m. Stop down to f/16 and the zone opens to 1.92 to 6.92 metres; at f/32, H is 2.65 metres, less than s, and the far limit is infinity.

    Depth of field prints all of this across twelve stops, with the exact power of the square root of two beside each marked one, and charts the zone of sharpness on a scale from half a metre to infinity, widening like a funnel as the lens closes.

    The limit of the small stop

    Stopping down cannot go on for ever. George Biddell Airy showed in 1835, in “On the Diffraction of an Object-glass with Circular Aperture”, that even a perfect lens draws a point as a bright disc ringed with fainter circles. Its radius to the first dark ring is 1.22λN, so its diameter is

    d = 2.44 × λ × N

    For green light at f/8 it is 2.44 × 0.00055 × 8 = 0.011 mm, a third of c. At f/22 it is 0.0295 mm, as large as c, and at f/64 it is 0.086 mm, nearly three times as large. That is why the stops on a lens for the 35 mm camera stop at f/16 or f/22. A negative 8 by 10 inches, printed by contact, can bear a circle of confusion of about 0.2 mm, and there f/64 is safe.

    The Californians knew it. On 15 November 1932 an exhibition opened at the M. H. de Young Memorial Museum in San Francisco by Ansel Adams, Imogen Cunningham, John Paul Edwards, Sonya Noskowiak, Henry Swift, Willard Van Dyke and Edward Weston, who called themselves Group f/64. Van Dyke later recalled suggesting “US 256”, the same stop in the Uniform System; Adams thought it would confuse the public. The small stop meant great depth of field and sharp detail from front to back on large negatives. With a 300 mm lens at f/64 and c = 0.2 mm, H is 7.3 metres. In August 1930 Weston had set a pepper inside a tin funnel and photographed it with his 8 × 10 camera and a Zeiss lens of 21 cm. His daybook records “an exposure of six minutes”.

    03 Aberration and design, 1839–1961

    Answering the aberrations

    A single lens draws a picture, but not a good one. The history of the photographic lens is a list of its faults and the glasses, one after another, that were put in their way.

    “It saw differently.”

    Five faults

    The thin-lens equation is true only for rays close to the axis. A real lens with spherical surfaces departs from it in ways that the Munich mathematician Ludwig von Seidel set out in 1856 as five aberrations: spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism, curvature of field and distortion. Glass adds a sixth fault, chromatic aberration, because it bends blue light more than red.

    Spherical aberration is the simplest to see. The rays that pass near the rim of a lens are bent too much, and cross the axis short of the focus of the rays near the centre. Take a lens with one face flat and the other curved with a radius R of 50 mm, in glass of index n = 1.5. Its focal length is

    f = R/(n - 1)

    that is, 100 mm. Open it to 12.5 mm either side of the axis, f/4. Spherical aberration traces ten rays through it by the law of refraction, sin i = n sin r, at each face. With the curved face to the light the rim rays cross 1.84 mm short of the focus of the central rays; turn the lens round, flat face first, and they fall 7.24 mm short, 3.9 times worse. The deck finds the smallest spot each way, 0.12 mm across and then 0.47 mm, and draws the beam from the side, narrowing to its waist and opening again. The fault grows as the square of the aperture: close to 6.25 mm, two stops, and it falls to a quarter.

    Colour is answered by pairing glasses. A typical crown glass has an Abbe number, a measure of how little it spreads the colours, of about 64, and a single lens of crown focuses blue and red apart by about f/V: for a 100 mm lens, 100/64 = 1.6 mm. A weaker negative lens of flint glass, which spreads colour more, cemented to a positive lens of crown, can bring two colours to one focus. Chester Moor Hall had made such doublets in the 1730s, and John Dollond was granted a patent for them in 1758.

    Landscape and portrait

    The first photographic lens was the achromatic landscape lens that Charles Chevalier made for the camera Alphonse Giroux sold with Daguerre’s process in 1839, a cemented doublet with a stop in front of it at about f/16. It drew a fair landscape, but slowly; a portrait sitter had to hold still for minutes.

    In 1840 Joseph Petzval, professor of mathematics in Vienna, computed something faster. Archduke Louis lent him eight gunners and three corporals of the artillery to do the arithmetic, and the lens, two doublets with a stop between them, was made by Peter Wilhelm Friedrich Voigtländer. It worked at f/3.6, and its gain over Chevalier’s lens is the ratio of the squares:

    (16/3.6)² = 19.8

    about twenty times the light, so that an exposure of ten minutes became one of thirty seconds. Voigtländer built it into an all-metal camera in 1841. The price was the field. Petzval’s lens was sharp in the middle and soft at the edges, where the image lies on a curved surface rather than the flat plate, and its useful field was only about 30 degrees.

    Three glasses, then four

    The cure for that curve came from Petzval’s own mathematics: the curvature of the field depends on a sum over the powers and indices of the elements, and can be brought to zero if positive and negative elements balance. In 1893 Harold Dennis Taylor, chief engineer of T. Cooke & Sons of York, patented the Cooke triplet: a negative element of flint between two positive elements of crown, spaced apart in air, so that the lens converged the light but drew on a flat field. With three elements it could correct all five of Seidel’s faults for one colour. Cooke was reluctant to make it, and the design was licensed to Taylor, Taylor and Hobson of Leicester.

    In 1902 Paul Rudolph at Carl Zeiss in Jena arrived by another road at the Tessar, four elements in three groups, the rear pair cemented. It began at f/6.3 and reached f/4.5 by 1907, and millions have been made since.

    Wide and long

    The focal length and the size of the frame together fix how much of the world a lens sees. Across the diagonal d of the frame, the angle of view is

    A = 2 × arctan(d/2f)

    The 24 × 36 frame has a diagonal of sqrt(24² + 36²) = 43.27 mm. A 50 mm lens sees 2 × arctan(0.433) = 46.8 degrees, and a lens whose focal length is close to the diagonal of its frame is called normal. A 28 mm lens sees 75.4 degrees, a 135 mm lens 18.2. The thin lens prints the figure for whatever lens and frame are on its first data card.

    Bill Brandt found a wider view in a second-hand shop near Covent Garden: an old wooden Kodak made to photograph the scenes of crimes. In his words, “Like nineteenth-century cameras it had no shutter, and the wide-angle lens, with an aperture as minute as a pinhole, was focused on infinity.” Its tiny stop made its depth of field almost endless, and its wide lens stretched whatever came near it. “My new camera saw more and it saw differently,” he wrote. His Perspective of Nudes followed in 1961.
