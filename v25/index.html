    FORTRAN and the punched card

    FORTRAN, short for formula translation, was created at IBM by a team led by John Backus. Its first compiler shipped in 1957 for the IBM 704, and it let scientists and engineers write their calculations as formulas instead of as machine instructions. The compiler produced code nearly as fast as code written by hand, which persuaded a sceptical profession that a high-level language could be practical. The language spread quickly. FORTRAN II followed in 1958 and FORTRAN IV in 1962, and the 1966 standard, FORTRAN 66, made FORTRAN one of the first programming languages with an official standard. The interpreter in this room runs a subset of that language.

    Programs of that era were not typed onto a screen. A programmer wrote the program in pencil on a coding form ruled in 80 columns, like the one on the desk, and the lines were then punched onto cards, one line to a card, on a keypunch such as the IBM 029, which IBM introduced in 1964 alongside its System/360 computers. As it punched each column, the 029 also printed the character along the top edge of the card, so that a person could read what a machine would read from the holes. A program was a stack of cards held together with a rubber band, and dropping a deck was a real hazard. That is why programmers punched sequence numbers into the last eight columns: a card sorter could put a scrambled deck back in order.

    FORTRAN gives each group of columns on a card its own job, and the coding form shades the groups so that you can see them.

    ColumnsWhat they hold
    1A C or an asterisk in column 1 turns the whole card into a comment.
    1–5An optional statement label: an unsigned number that other statements can refer to. Blanks within the label are ignored.
    6Any character other than a blank or a zero marks the card as a continuation of the statement on the card before.
    7–72The statement itself.
    73–80The compiler ignores these columns, which traditionally held sequence numbers. The interpreter warns you if anything is punched in them.

    A statement too long for one card goes on as many continuation cards as it needs: columns 7–72 of each continuation card are joined to the statement in the order the cards come.

    FORTRAN ignores blanks outside of text, so G O T O 20 means the same thing as GOTO 20. The rule has a famous consequence. FORTRAN has no reserved words, so DO 10 I = 1, 10 begins a loop, but DO 10 I = 1.10, with a full stop where the comma should be, assigns 1.1 to a variable named DO10I. The coding form accepts only the characters that the 029 keypunch could punch, and it turns lowercase letters into capitals, because the keypunch had no lowercase.

    The holes in the card

    The punched card goes back to Herman Hollerith, whose cards and tabulating machines counted the United States census of 1890. IBM’s 80-column card, with its narrow rectangular holes, dates from 1928, and it was still the commonest way into a computer in the 1960s.

    A card has 80 columns and 12 rows. From the top, the rows are called 12, 11, 0 and then 1 to 9. Each column holds one character, and the character is the set of holes punched in that column. The card in hand at the keypunch shows the holes, and prints each character above its column, as the 029 did.

    CharactersHoles
    BlankNo holes at all.
    0–9One hole, in the row of that digit.
    A–IA hole in row 12 and a hole in rows 1 to 9: A is 12-1, I is 12-9.
    J–RA hole in row 11 and a hole in rows 1 to 9: J is 11-1, R is 11-9.
    S–ZA hole in row 0 and a hole in rows 2 to 9: S is 0-2, Z is 0-9.
    SignsMost signs add a hole in row 8. The full stop is 12-8-3, the comma 0-8-3, the left parenthesis 12-8-5, the right parenthesis 11-8-5, the asterisk 11-8-4, the plus sign 12-8-6, the equals sign 8-6 and the apostrophe 8-5. The minus sign is a lone 11, the ampersand a lone 12, and the slash 0-1.

    Rows 12, 11 and 0 are called the zone rows, because a hole in one of them chooses a group of characters, and the digit row then chooses one character within the group; row 0 serves as a digit too. So GO is punched as 12-7 in one column and 11-6 in the next. The letters S to Z start at row 2; 0-1 is the slash. The 029 could punch 64 characters in all. A FORTRAN program needs only the letters, the digits, the blank and the signs = + - * / ( ) , . $ '; the rest serve comments and printed headings.

    Volume 1 · Chapter 3

    The deck

    A job is a single deck of cards. The program comes first and finishes with an END card, and every card after the END card is data for the program to read. On an IBM System/360 the deck would also have needed job-control cards ahead of the program, to tell the operating system who was paying and which compiler to run; here they are not needed. Blank cards at the end of the deck are not sent to the reader.

    Each READ takes the next data card, so the data cards must come in the order the program reads them. If the program tries to read past the last data card, the job stops with the message END OF FILE ON UNIT 5, just as it would have on an IBM machine.

    A data card is read in fields of fixed width, as the program’s FORMAT statement lays them out. The data card of the second sample deck, Heron’s formula, holds the three sides of a triangle under FORMAT (3I5): the first side in columns 1–5, the second in columns 6–10 and the third in columns 11–15. Numbers are best punched at the right of their fields. Blanks before and after a number are ignored, but a blank between digits reads as a zero, so 1 2 in a field reads as 102, as it did on the IBM machines. An all-blank field reads as 0. Under Fw.d, a number punched without a decimal point takes d decimal places from the format, so 314 read with F5.2 is 3.14; a point punched in the field overrides the format.

    Using the room

    The workroom is seen straight on. This manual stands on the bookshelf in three volumes, beside the magazine in its ledge, the Xenakis miscellanea in a file box and the deck box. On the desk below are the coding form, the out tray, the computer and the tape player. Under the desk stand the card reader and line printer, through which every deck that is run must pass, and the UPIC, a tablet on which music is drawn rather than written.

    Taking a thing up
    Whatever can be taken up is outlined in white under the pointer, with a caption beside it that says what pressing it does. Press it to take it up, or choose it from the Menu in the top corner of the page. From the keyboard, the arrow keys move from one thing to the nearest in that direction, Enter takes it up, and H outlines everything in the room that can be taken up.
    Looking
    To look at a thing instead, press it with the right button, or press L with it under the pointer or focused. A line along the foot of the room says what it is. Each print on the wall also offers a deck it calls to mind, to put on the coding form.
    Swapping
    The prints, the plants, the lamp and the mug can be changed: pressing one puts another of its kind in its place. Pressing the bare wall, or Change the wallpaper in the menu, hangs another paper.
    The switch and the light
    The switch on the wall turns the lamp off and on. The light follows the clock of your own computer, so the room is seen by morning, evening or night; Light in the menu sets one of the three instead.
    Sounds
    The room has its own small sounds: the lamp’s hum, the keypunch, the reader and the printer, the tape motor. Room sounds in the menu turns them off and on. Nothing sounds until you first press something in the page.
    Keeping a room
    The room is furnished by chance each time the page is opened. The furnishing is written into the page’s address as it changes, so Copy a link to this room in the menu gives a link that opens the same room for someone else. Nothing is stored.
    Putting a thing down
    Pressing Escape, except while typing on the coding form, puts the object down and returns to the room, and so does Back to the room in the corner. In this manual, the contents list and the Previous page and Next page buttons turn the chapters, and so do the left and right arrow keys.

    The deck box holds every sample deck, each behind a tab with its name, in three divisions: the programs, the music decks, and the decks printed in the magazine, which the magazine’s articles can also put on the form. Taking out a deck puts it on the coding form in place of the cards that were there.

    On the coding form you can also punch your own cards. Pressing Enter adds a new card below the current one, pressing Backspace on an empty card removes it, and the up and down arrow keys move between cards. The Add card, Delete card and Clear deck buttons do the same with the pointer. If you paste several lines at once, each line becomes a card of its own. When the deck is ready, press Run deck.

    Under the form, at the keypunch, lies the card in hand: the card selected on the form, shown with its holes. As you type, the punch head follows the column being punched, and each new column shows its holes at once. The Previous card and Next card buttons step through the deck, and the form’s selection follows along.

    A deck that is run goes first through the card reader and line printer under the desk (see The card reader), and its printout then lands in the out tray. The out tray holds the printer output, the job log with any errors and warnings, a listing of the program, and the output stacker, where the cards that the program punched land. The stacker is emptied at the start of each run. From the stacker you can put the cards on the coding form in place of the deck, add them to the deck as data for another run, copy them or download them as text, thread them onto the tape player, or read them into the computer.

    The computer on the desk has a desktop of its own, with an Editor in place of the coding form, files in place of decks, a Player, a course in punched cards, a calculator, a game, folders of miscellanea, and its own Read Me. The keypunch and the computer share their programs. Type into the computer, under the coding form, opens the deck in the computer’s Editor as text, one line to a card, and Read into the computer, at the stacker, opens the punched cards there. In the computer, Punch on Cards in the File menu sends the Editor’s text back to the coding form, one card to a line. Back to the Room, in the computer’s first menu, or the Back to the room button in the corner, leaves the computer as it is; it keeps its windows and files until the page is reloaded.

    The deck on the form is kept only while the page is open, so reloading the page brings back the first sample.

    The language

    The interpreter understands a single main program; subroutines and functions are not supported. A name has up to six letters and digits and must start with a letter. Unless you declare otherwise, names that begin with the letters I through N hold integers, and all other names hold real numbers. Integers are 32 bits wide, and integer division throws away the remainder, so 7/2 is 3 and -7/2 is −3. Where an integer and a real meet in an expression, the integer is made real. Real numbers have single precision. A variable that has not been given a value holds zero.

    StatementWhat it does
    V = exprThis evaluates the expression and stores the result in V, which can be a variable or an array element, converting it to the type of V. Expressions use + - * / and ** for powers, with parentheses for grouping.
    INTEGER, REALThese declare names as integers or reals, overriding the first-letter rule.
    DIMENSION A(n), B(m,n)This declares arrays of one or two dimensions, numbered from 1.
    GO TO nThis jumps to the statement labelled n. The computed form GO TO (n1,n2,...), I jumps to the Ith label in the list; if I is out of range, it goes on to the next statement.
    IF (e) n1, n2, n3This jumps to n1, n2, or n3 when e is negative, zero, or positive.
    IF (cond) stmtThis runs stmt only when the condition is true. Conditions compare values with .LT. .LE. .EQ. .NE. .GT. .GE. and combine them with .AND. .OR. .NOT.; .TRUE. and .FALSE. are the two logical constants.
    DO n I = m1, m2, m3This repeats the statements through label n while I counts from m1 to m2 in steps of m3, or in steps of 1 if m3 is left out. As on most compilers of the time, the loop always runs at least once, even when m1 is already past m2.
    CONTINUEThis does nothing. It usually marks the end of a DO loop.
    READ (5,f) list or READ f, listThis reads the next data card, laid out as FORMAT statement f describes.
    WRITE (6,f) list or PRINT f, listThis prints a line on the line printer.
    PUNCH f, list or WRITE (7,f) listThis punches a new card into the output stacker.
    FORMAT (...)This describes the layout of a card or a printed line. Iw is an integer w columns wide, Fw.d and Ew.d are real numbers with d digits after the point, nX skips n columns, nHtext and 'text' are literal text, and a slash starts a new line or card. Descriptors can be repeated, as in 3I5, or grouped, as in 2(I3,F6.2).
    STOP, PAUSE, ENDSTOP ends the job, PAUSE writes a note in the job log and carries on, and END marks the last card of the program. A number after STOP or PAUSE, as in STOP 3, is written in the job log.

    The built-in functions are SQRT, ABS, IABS, FLOAT, INT, IFIX, MOD, AMOD, SIN, COS, ATAN, EXP, ALOG, ALOG10, MAX0, MIN0, AMAX1, AMIN1, and SIGN. As the names show, a function that returns an integer begins with I to N, and one that returns a real does not: FLOAT makes an integer real, and IFIX and INT cut a real back to an integer.

    Some notes on FORMAT. A FORMAT statement may stand anywhere in the program, before or after the statements that use it. Printed numbers are set at the right of their fields, and a number too wide for its field prints as a row of asterisks, so 12345 under I3 prints ***. E12.4 prints 12345.0 as 0.1235E+05. If items in the list remain when the format reaches its closing parenthesis, a new line or card begins and the format starts again from its last group in parentheses, or from the beginning if it has none. nH text, the Hollerith field, is named after Herman Hollerith; its count must match the characters that follow exactly, blanks included. The apostrophe form was an IBM extension that later became standard.

    Errors are reported in the job log with the number of the card they occur on: an unknown statement, unbalanced parentheses, an undefined label, a missing FORMAT, a wrong Hollerith count, a DO loop whose last statement cannot be found, a subscript out of range, an integer division by zero, or data that cannot be read. A program that runs a million statements without stopping is halted with TIME LIMIT EXCEEDED, as a computing centre would cut off a job that had used up its time.

    Volume 1 · Chapter 6

    The card reader

    At a computing centre, the programmer did not run a deck. The deck was handed in at a counter, the operators fed it through a card reader with the decks of other users, and the printout came back, often hours later, folded round the cards. Readers were fast: the IBM 2540 card read punch, which served many System/360 installations, read 1,000 cards a minute.

    The card reader and line printer under the desk stand in for all of that. Pressing Run deck on the coding form sends the deck into the reader’s hopper, and the reader takes the cards one by one: the deck in the hopper goes down, the lamp is lit, and each card shows at the read station as it passes, with a count of the cards read so far. Then the printer prints the job’s output a line at a time on its fan-fold paper, with a rule across the paper wherever the program begins a new page.

    The whole deck takes no more than about three seconds to read, and the printout no more than about four to print. Skip to the end of the job finishes both at once; if your computer is set to reduce motion, the job is over as soon as it starts. When the printer stops, the reader says whether the job ran without errors or stopped with errors, and Take the printout to the out tray carries it to the desk, where the job log, the listing and any punched cards wait with it. Go to the coding form returns to the deck instead.

    The line printer

    Results came back on a line printer such as the IBM 1403, which printed a whole line at a time onto continuous fan-fold paper. A chain carrying several copies of the character set spun past a row of hammers, one hammer for each print position, and each hammer fired the moment its character went by. The 1403 printed lines of up to 132 characters, at 600 lines a minute in its early models. The printer here takes lines of up to 132 characters as well; a longer line is cut short, with a warning in the job log.

    The first character of each printed line is never printed. Instead, it tells the printer how far to move the paper before printing.

    First characterWhat the printer does
    BlankMoves the paper one line.
    0Leaves one blank line first.
    -Leaves two blank lines first.
    1Starts a new page.
    +Does not move the paper, and prints over the line before.

    That is why a printing FORMAT usually begins with 1X or 1H , giving the printer a blank to swallow. The rule caused famous mistakes. If a FORMAT happens to put a 1 in the first position, the printer throws a new page, so printing 123 with I3 starts a new page and prints only 23. With I5 the number is printed with two blanks before it, and the first of them is taken as carriage control. Punched cards have no carriage control: on a card, the first character of the record is column 1.

    Iannis Xenakis and the sieve

    Iannis Xenakis (1922–2001) was a Greek-French composer, architect, and engineer, born in Romania to a Greek family. He came to Paris as a political refugee in 1947. He then spent twelve years in Le Corbusier’s studio, where he took charge of designing the Philips Pavilion for the 1958 Brussels World’s Fair, whose curved shells grew out of the same ruled surfaces that the string glissandi of his Metastaseis trace on a graph. He brought an engineer’s habits of mind to music. He composed with probability and set theory, and he was one of the first composers to write music with a computer. In 1962 he ran his Free Stochastic Music program on an IBM 7090 at IBM France in Paris. Xenakis designed the program, and M. F. Génuys and M. J. Barraud coded it in FORTRAN IV. Its output became the ST pieces, among them ST/10 and ST/4, whose full titles record the date the data was generated: 8 February 1962, or 080262. He published the program several times, most completely in his book Formalized Music (1971).

    During a residency in Berlin in 1963–64, Xenakis conceived his theory of sieves, a way of building scales and rhythms out of simple arithmetic. A sieve is made from residual classes. The class written here as m@s (Xenakis wrote m with a subscript s) contains every integer x for which x − s is divisible by m, so 3@0 is the sequence …, 0, 3, 6, 9, …, and 5@2 is …, 2, 7, 12, 17, …. The modulus m sets the spacing and the shift s sets the starting point, so 3@4 is the same class as 3@1.

    Combining classes by union, intersection, and complement produces patterns that repeat exactly but sound irregular. This manual writes the union, the numbers in at least one class, with a bar, as in 3@0 | 4@1. The intersection, the numbers in every class, is written with an ampersand, and the complement, the numbers in no class, with an exclamation mark. Every sieve repeats after the least common multiple of its moduli: 3@0 | 4@1 repeats every 12, and its members in one period are 0, 1, 3, 5, 6 and 9, with the uneven gaps 1, 2, 2, 1 and 3, and then 3 again to reach 12.

    He first used sieves in Akrata (1964–65), for sixteen wind instruments, and in Nomos Alpha (1965–66) for solo cello they select the pitches from a scale of quarter-tones. He set the theory out in “Vers une métamusique” (1967), later a chapter of Formalized Music, and returned to it in an article, “Sieves”, in Perspectives of New Music in 1990.

    The sieve generator deck

    The fifth deck among the programs in the deck box is a sieve generator written in the same FORTRAN that the interpreter runs, in the spirit of Xenakis’s own programs. The first data card after END holds three numbers in 5-column fields. MODE is 1 for the union of the classes, 2 for their intersection, or 3 for the complement of their union. N is the number of classes, from 1 to 10, and LIMIT sets the range, because the program tests every integer from 0 to LIMIT − 1. Each of the next N cards describes one class with a modulus M and a shift S, again in 5-column fields. The shift may be negative: 3@-1 is the same class as 3@2.

    For each integer in the range, the program counts how many of the classes contain it, and the mode decides from the count whether it belongs to the sieve. FORTRAN’s MOD takes the sign of its first argument, so MOD(-1,3) is −1, not 2; the deck therefore computes MOD(MOD(NV-IS(K),IM(K))+IM(K),IM(K)), which is always between 0 and m − 1. Only one flat combination can be made in a run, not a nested one such as (3@0 | 4@1) & !5@2.

    The program prints each member of the sieve along with its distance from the member before it, and then the number of members. It punches each member onto a card of its own, in columns 1–5, so that you can feed the members to another program as data. The sample data describes the sieve 5@2 | 5@3 over the integers 0 to 19:

    CardPunchedMeaning
    1    1    2   20Union of 2 classes, tested on 0 to 19.
    2    5    2The class 5@2.
    3    5    3The class 5@3.

    The sieve contains 2, 3, 7, 8, 12, 13, 17, and 18, and the intervals alternate 1 and 4, because the sieve repeats every 5. Some other data to try: the union of 3@0 and 4@0 below 12 gives 0, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 9; their intersection below 24 gives only 0 and 12, the multiples of 12; and the complement of 3@0 below 7 gives 1, 2, 4 and 5. To see the idea in numbers rather than notes, change the three data cards at the bottom of the deck and run it again. To hear it in notes, thread the punched members onto the tape player.

    Volume 3 · Chapter 10

    The music decks

    The music decks are the second division in the deck box. Each one is a FORTRAN program that punches a card for every note, in the layout that the tape player reads, and each one follows an idea of Xenakis’s. Their data cards come after the END card, and the comment cards at the top of each deck explain what the numbers mean, so you can change them and run the deck again. Most of the decks also print a short account of what they made, such as the scale they built or the first cycle of a rhythm.

    Sieve scale (a melody)
    This deck takes the union of several residual classes over the semitones above a base note. The result is a scale that need not repeat at the octave, and a walk by chance on that scale, up to two steps up or down at a time, makes a melody. The data cards give the number of classes, the range in semitones and the base note, then the classes, then a seed for the chances and the number of notes; a different seed gives a different walk on the same scale.
    Two rhythm sieves
    Two sieves over a cycle of pulses strike a low drum and a high one, and the stroke is louder where they fall together. The data cards set the length of the cycle and the number of cycles, then each drum’s note and its classes. Xenakis built the rhythms of Psappha (1975), for solo percussion, from sieves.
    Stochastic cloud (after Achorripsis)
    Notes arrive at random at a fixed mean rate, in a band of pitch that rises three octaves over the cloud, and the printout sets the counts of notes in each cell of four pulses against the Poisson law. In Achorripsis (1956–57), for 21 instruments, Xenakis used the Poisson law with λ = 0.6 to decide how many events fall in each cell of a matrix of 28 units of time by 7 timbres.
    Markov melody (after Analogique)
    A table of chances decides which of five notes follows each note, which makes the melody a Markov chain. Row i of the table gives, in percent, the chances of going from the ith note to each of the five, so editing a row changes the character of the tune. Xenakis used Markov chains in Analogique A (1958), for nine strings, and Analogique B (1959), for sine tones on tape.
    String glissandi (after Metastaseis)
    Up to eight lines each slide in semitone steps from a note in a cluster to a note in a wide chord, and all the lines take the same time. In bars 309–314 of Metastaseis (1953–54), Xenakis drew the string glissandi as straight lines on a graph of pitch against time.

    Volume 3 · Chapter 11

    The tape player

    The tape player plays the cards in the output stacker as sound. A tape card holds four numbers in 5-column fields, as FORMAT (4I5) punches them.

    ColumnsFieldMeaning
    1–5TIMEThe onset in pulses, counted from 0.
    6–10NOTEA MIDI note number from 0 to 127, where 60 is middle C and each step is a semitone.
    11–15LENThe length in pulses. A blank or a 0 means 1.
    16–20LOUDThe loudness from 1 to 9. A blank or a 0 means 7, and anything above 9 counts as 9.

    So the card     8   67    4    9 plays the G above middle C on the ninth pulse, for one beat, as loud as the player goes. A pulse is a quarter of a beat, so at the starting tempo of 120 beats a minute a pulse lasts an eighth of a second. Notes play in the order of their times, and cards with the same time keep their card order.

    A card with only its first five columns punched, such as a card from the sieve generator, holds a bare number. With the Rhythm switch, the player plays each bare number as a time, so a sieve sounds as a rhythm on middle C. With the Scale switch, it plays the numbers one per pulse in card order, as semitones above the C an octave below middle C, so a sieve sounds as a scale. The switch matters only for bare numbers: the music decks punch full tape cards that set their own times and notes, so for their tapes the switch cannot be moved. A card is skipped if any of its fields is not a whole number, if anything is punched past column 20, if its time is blank or negative, or if it holds more than a time but no note, or a note outside 0 to 127. Cards of text are skipped this way, and the player says how many it skipped.

    Press Thread onto the tape player at the stacker, or Thread tape from the stacker on the tape player, to wind the stacker’s cards onto the reels, and then press Play. Pause holds the place, and Stop rewinds the tape to the start; the counter shows the place in minutes and seconds. The tempo control sets the beats per minute, from 40 to 240. The speed switch changes the tape speed from 7½ to 15 inches per second, two standard speeds of studio recorders, which, as on a real machine, plays the music twice as fast and an octave higher.

    Each note sounds in the wave chosen on the deck’s Wave pad. Tape, the player’s own voice, is a triangle wave with a quiet tone an octave above it; Sine, Triangle, Reed and Saw sound in those shapes alone; and a cycle drawn on the pad with the pointer sounds as it is drawn, as the UPIC’s waves do. A new wave takes effect from the next note. Whatever the wave, the player adds a slow wobble in pitch, the “wow” of a tape that runs not quite evenly, a softened treble, and a faint hiss while the tape runs, as a tape recording would have. Sound starts only when you press Play.

    Volume 3 · Chapter 12

    The UPIC

    The UPIC, the Unité Polyagogique Informatique du CEMAMu, was a system for composing by drawing, devised by Xenakis and completed in 1977 at the CEMAMu, his centre for mathematical and automatic music in Paris. The composer drew on a large tablet linked to a computer. Waveforms and envelopes could be drawn, and then the music itself, with time running across the page and pitch rising up it, so that a line became a sound that changes its pitch as the line climbs or falls. Xenakis made Mycènes Alpha (1978) on it.

    The tablet under the desk is made after the UPIC. On its page, time runs to the right and pitch rises up it over four octaves, from C2 at the foot to C6 at the top, with a darker rule at every C. The faint vertical lines mark the seconds; Length of the page sets how many seconds the page lasts, from 4 to 40.

    Drawing
    Draw on the page with the pointer. Each line is a voice: a level line holds a note, a slanting line is a glissando, a line that branches is an arborescence, and a scatter of dots is a cloud of short notes. A line runs only forward in time, as on the UPIC, so a stroke drawn back to the left is passed over. From the keyboard, the arrow keys move the pen and Space puts it down and lifts it again; the status line names the time and the note under the pen. Take off the last line and Clear the page undo the work.
    The wave
    Every voice sounds in the wave on the small pad: one cycle of it, which you can draw freehand or choose as Sine, Triangle, Reed or Saw. A smooth wave sounds pure, and a wave with corners sounds bright and buzzing.
    Pages to begin from
    Three pages are ready to play or change: an arborescence after Mycènes Alpha, crossing glissandi, and a cloud of points.
    Playing
    Play the page runs a playhead across it, and each line sounds, and is drawn in red, while the playhead crosses it.
    Punching
    Punch the page as tape cards reads each line at every pulse, an eighth of a second at 120 beats a minute, as the nearest semitone, and punches a tape card for each note, with a note held over several pulses on a single card with its length. The cards go into the out tray’s stacker in place of what was there, up to 999 of them, and Thread them on the tape player winds them onto the reels. On tape, a glissando becomes a run of semitone steps, played in whatever wave is chosen on the tape player’s own pad (the UPIC’s wave is not punched with the page).

    From the editors

    This issue is about the early meeting of art and computing, the years when a picture, a poem or a score could come out of a deck of punched cards and a line printer. Its four articles follow pictures made by programs; works left to chance or written as instructions for someone else to carry out; machines that do nothing but follow rules; and rules that loop, wear away and return.

    None of it is only to be read. Wherever an article discusses a deck, a button beside the text puts that deck on the coding form. Run it, and the card reader takes the cards one by one, the line printer answers line by line, and the printout lands in the out tray, often with a few freshly punched cards in the stacker beside it: a picture printed in two characters, a poem drawn from a hat, the tape of a machine that has stopped, or the last generation of a pattern, ready to be fed back in. The catalogue at the back lists all thirteen decks in the order the articles take them up.

    Every deck is written in the same FORTRAN as the rest of the desk. The program comes first; its data cards follow the END card; and the comment cards at the top, the ones with a C in column 1, say what each number on the data cards means. Those numbers are there to be changed. A different seed gives a different poem, a different screen gives a different picture, a different table gives a different machine, and the same seed always gives the same result again, which is the whole difference between chance and noise.

    A word on the decks that use chance. A computer cannot shake a hat, so each one keeps a single number and, at every draw, multiplies it by 171 and keeps the remainder after dividing by 30269. The run of remainders looks random enough to choose words and notes, yet it is completely determined by the first number, the seed, which is why a seed on a data card can be written down, passed on and played back.

    Punch them, alter them, and leave the results in the tray for the next person at the desk.

    Printed in cyan, magenta, yellow and black, with a spot orange.

    01 Computer graphics, 1965–1968

    Pictures from programs

    In 1965 three galleries hung pictures that no hand had drawn. Within three years computer art had a museum show, a front-page scandal and a London exhibition seen by tens of thousands.

    Not one picture was sold.

    Stuttgart, February 1965

    The first exhibition of pictures generated by a digital computer opened on 4 February 1965 in the Studiengalerie of the Technische Hochschule Stuttgart and ran from 5 to 19 February. It showed the work of one man, Georg Nees, a mathematician at Siemens in Erlangen, and it was held under the auspices of the aesthetics colloquium led by the philosopher Max Bense. Nees had helped his data centre acquire a flatbed plotter, the Zuse Graphomat Z64, which drew in ink under the control of punched tape, and he wrote his own libraries in ALGOL to drive it and to supply it with random numbers. For the opening Bense and Nees issued a slim booklet, number 19 of Bense’s series rot, titled Computer-Grafik; Bense’s short text in it, Projekte generativer Ästhetik, is often read as the first manifesto of computer art.

    New York, April 1965

    Two months later, from 6 to 24 April, the Howard Wise Gallery in New York showed Computer-Generated Pictures by Béla Julesz and A. Michael Noll of Bell Telephone Laboratories. Their pictures had been computed on an IBM 7094 and drawn by a General Dynamics SC-4020 microfilm plotter; some were stereo pairs that sprang into depth through glasses handed out at the gallery. The invitation was a deck of four punched cards in different colours. Not one picture was sold.

    Noll had already tested how such pictures were seen. In 1964 he programmed a pseudo-random imitation of Piet Mondrian’s Composition with Lines (1917) and showed copies of both to a hundred people at Bell Labs. Only 28 per cent picked out the Mondrian, and 59 per cent preferred the computer’s version, calling it neater and more varied.

    In November 1965, from the 5th to the 26th, the bookseller Wendelin Niedlich showed Nees together with a young mathematician, Frieder Nake, in his Stuttgart gallery, and Bense opened it. It was the third exhibition of its kind in the world, and all three had happened in a single year.

    A nude in symbols

    At Bell Labs, Kenneth Knowlton and Leon Harmon made the picture that took the idea out of the laboratory. They scanned a photograph of the dancer and choreographer Deborah Hay, reduced it to a grid of grey values, and replaced each cell with one of a small set of symbols, drawn from mathematics and electronics, whose density of ink matched the grey. Up close the print is a field of tiny signs; from across the room it is a reclining figure. Collections date it to 1966 or 1967 and title it Studies in Perception I; it is also known as Computer Nude. The print was about 12 feet wide, and its first public outing was as a prank, pinned up in a colleague’s office. On 10 October 1967 it hung behind the speakers at the press conference in Robert Rauschenberg’s loft that launched Experiments in Art and Technology, and the next day it was in The New York Times. The Museum of Modern Art showed it in The Machine as Seen at the End of the Mechanical Age (25 November 1968 to 9 February 1969).

    The same trick with two marks

    The idea behind Studies in Perception is the halftone, in which a newspaper prints a photograph as dots whose size follows its tone. The deck Studies in perception carries it out with the only two marks this line printer can be asked for: a full stop for paper and an asterisk for ink.

    It builds its picture cell by cell, 64 characters wide and 32 lines deep, and gives every cell a tone from 0 (paper) to 16 (solid ink). The picture is this page’s own: a diagonal wash that darkens toward the lower right, a target of dark and light rings on the left, and a solid black disc on the right. Printer characters are taller than they are wide, so the deck stretches its circles sideways to make them come out round on the paper.

    Then it decides where the ink goes. A data card holds a 4-by-4 screen of sixteen thresholds, the numbers 0 to 15 arranged so that neighbouring cells are as different as possible, the pattern Bryce Bayer of Eastman Kodak described in 1973. Every cell of the picture is compared with the threshold that falls on it as the screen is repeated across the page, and inked wherever its tone is higher. A tone of 1 inks one cell in sixteen, a tone of 8 inks half of them in an even checker, and 16 inks everything. The method is called ordered dithering, and it is how a two-level device fakes a grey.

    What to try: the first data card sets the width of the rings in lines, so a 1 crowds them and a 4 makes a bold target. The second card is the screen itself. Replace it with sixteen thresholds that grow outward from the middle of the square, and the dots clump into a coarse newspaper screen instead of the fine, even Bayer texture. Every row is also punched as 64 digits, 1 for ink and 0 for paper, so the stacker ends up holding the picture as 32 cards of pure data.

    London, 1968

    From 2 August to 20 October 1968, Cybernetic Serendipity at the Institute of Contemporary Arts in London, curated by Jasia Reichardt, set out to show computer-aided creative work of every kind: graphics, music, poetry, dance, sculpture and film, along with painting machines, robots and works in which chance played a large part. It had more than 130 contributors, from composers and engineers to poets, and was seen by some 60,000 visitors. The record issued with it included an excerpt of Iannis Xenakis’s Stratégie (1962), alongside Lejaren Hiller and Leonard Isaacson’s Illiac Suite and John Cage’s Cartridge Music. In three years, pictures from programs had gone from a university seminar room to a public that queued to see them.

    02 Dada, Duchamp and Fluxus

    Chance and instructions

    Long before there were computers, artists handed the making of a work to chance, or to a set of instructions someone else would carry out. A program is both at once.

    “The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

    A poem from a bag

    In 1920 the Dada poet Tristan Tzara included a recipe in his Dada manifesto on feeble love and bitter love. To make a Dadaist poem, take a newspaper and a pair of scissors; choose an article as long as the poem you want; cut it out, then cut out each of its words and put them in a bag; shake gently; take the cuttings out one after another and copy them down conscientiously in the order they left the bag. “The poem will resemble you,” he promised.

    Tzara’s hat follows the recipe to the letter. Its newspaper item is sixteen words long, a small news story from the computing centre itself: THE CARD READER SWALLOWED A DECK OF FORTY HOLES AND THE PRINTER ANSWERED IN CAPITALS NOBODY. The deck shakes the bag by shuffling the sixteen words, then draws them one at a time and prints each on its own line. Now and then, at random, the hand pauses and a stanza ends. Every word drawn is also punched on a card of its own, so the cuttings pile up in the stacker in the order they left the bag, and the run closes with Tzara’s promise, printed in capitals. With the seed on the data card, 191, the poem begins A / OF / FORTY / SWALLOWED; change the seed and the same sixteen words resemble someone else. The second number on the card says how many words to draw, so a poem can stop before the bag is empty.

    Notes from a hat

    Marcel Duchamp tried the same thing with music seven years earlier. On a New Year’s visit to the family home in Rouen in 1913, he and his sisters Yvonne and Magdeleine, both musicians, each drew twenty-five notes out of a hat, from the F below middle C up to high F, and wrote them down in the order they came. The result was Erratum musical, a song for three voices whose parts are headed with their three names, sung to a dictionary’s definition of the verb imprimer, to print. Duchamp later published the score in the Green Box of 1934.

    Erratum musical makes three sets of twenty-five cards, one note on each, numbered as on a modern keyboard from 53 to 77, mixes each set in the hat and draws it out. It prints each voice as a list of draws with the note’s number and name (F3, C#5 and so on), and punches every note drawn as a card holding the voice and the note. Duchamp himself moved some of the highest notes in his own part down to suit a man’s voice; the deck leaves them where the hat put them. The seed on the data card is 1913.

    The canned metre

    In Paris in 1913–14 Duchamp made 3 standard stoppages. His note for it reads: a straight horizontal thread one metre long falls from a height of one metre onto a horizontal plane, twisting as it pleases, and creates a new image of the unit of length. He did it three times, fixed each thread with varnish to a strip of canvas, and had wooden straightedges cut to the three curves. He called the result canned chance, and used the curves again in Network of Stoppages (1914).

    The deck 3 standard stoppages drops its threads as twenty pieces of 5 centimetres, each turned by chance from the direction of the one before, by up to the angle on the data card (0.35 radians, about twenty degrees). It prints the position of the thread every fourth piece, as it lands, and then measures the straight distance from one end to the other: that is the thread’s new metre. With the seed 1914 the three new metres come out at roughly 81, 97 and 96 centimetres, and all three are punched. Raise the largest turn and the threads curl up and the metres shrink; set it to zero and every metre is a hundred centimetres exactly.

    Scores made of sentences

    In the late 1950s and the 1960s, artists around Fluxus wrote works as short instructions for someone else to carry out. George Brecht, who had studied with John Cage, began writing what he called event scores in 1959; Yoko Ono collected more than 150 instruction works in her book Grapefruit (1964). La Monte Young’s Composition 1960 #10, dedicated to Bob Morris, reads in full: “Draw a straight line and follow it.” At the Fluxus festival in Wiesbaden in September 1962, Nam June Paik performed it by dipping his head in ink and drawing the line with his head, hands and necktie down a long sheet of paper on the floor, a performance now known as Zen for Head. In “Paragraphs on Conceptual Art” (Artforum, June 1967), Sol LeWitt described the same handing-over of decisions: “The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

    The oldest instruction piece

    A program is an instruction piece performed by a machine, and Euclid, read aloud performs one of the oldest. In Book VII of the Elements, written about 300 BC, Euclid shows how to find the greatest common measure of two numbers by taking the smaller from the larger, over and over, until they agree. Donald Knuth called it “the granddaddy of all algorithms”, the oldest nontrivial one still in use. The deck uses the remainder, which does a whole run of subtractions at once: replace the pair M, N by N and the remainder of M divided by N, until the remainder is 0.

    What makes the deck a performance is that it reads its own score aloud. As it carries out each card, it prints the card on the left of the page and what happened on the right: M IS 1071, N IS 462; K IS 147; AND AGAIN. With 1071 and 462 on the data card it goes round three times (1071 leaves 147 after 462, 462 leaves 21 after 147, and 21 goes into 147 exactly) and announces the answer, 21, before printing THE PIECE IS OVER.

    It also leaves a second score behind. While it runs, it punches a new program: the steps it actually took, with the two starting numbers written in, every test already decided and the loop written out in full. Load the stacker in place of the deck and run it, and the new program prints THE GREATEST COMMON DIVISOR IS 21 without making a single decision. The first deck is the instruction; the second is one performance of it, written down.

    03 Computation theory

    Machines that follow rules

    A tape, a head and a table of instructions are enough to compute anything that can be computed, and enough to ask questions that nothing can answer. A row or a grid of cells is enough to make patterns nobody has finished understanding.

    Which one leaves the most 1s behind?

    A machine on paper

    In 1936 Alan Turing described a machine that does nothing but read and write symbols on a tape, one square at a time, following a table of instructions. In each state, the table says, for the symbol under the head, what to write, which way to move and which state comes next. Turing argued that such a machine can carry out any calculation that a person following fixed rules can, and that one machine, given the table of another on its tape, can imitate it. His paper “On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem” was received by the London Mathematical Society on 28 May 1936 and read on 12 November 1936. It also proved that some questions about such machines can never be settled by any machine, most famously whether a given one will ever stop.

    The busy beaver game

    In May 1962 Tibor Radó, a mathematician at Ohio State University, turned that limit into something that sounds like a game, in “On Non-Computable Functions” in the Bell System Technical Journal. Consider every machine with a given number of states and two symbols, 0 and 1, that starts on a blank tape and eventually halts. Which one leaves the most 1s behind? Radó called the winners busy beavers, and he proved that no program can find them for every number of states: the numbers grow faster than anything a program can compute.

    Small cases can still be settled by hand and machine together. Shen Lin and Radó settled three states in 1965: the most any three-state machine can leave is six 1s, and no three-state machine that halts runs for more than 21 steps. Allen Brady settled four states in 1983, with 13 ones and 107 steps. Five states took until 2024, when an online collaboration, the Busy Beaver Challenge, proved that the longest a five-state machine can run and still halt is 47,176,870 steps, and checked the proof with the Coq proof assistant.

    A busy beaver runs a three-state champion on a tape of 24 cells, starting in the middle. At every step it prints the step number, the state (A, B or C, and H once it halts), the whole tape, and the count of 1s, with a row of dots underneath that marks where the head is. Watch the head shuttle back and forth laying down 1s; after 14 steps it halts with six of them in a row, and the final tape is punched.

    The machine itself is data. Its table sits on the cards after END, one card per state, and the comment cards give Brady’s four-state champion card by card: put it in, set the first card to four states, start cell 16 and a limit of 200 steps, and watch it run for 107 steps and leave thirteen 1s. Invent a table of your own and the deck will tell you whether it halted, ran off the end of the tape, or was still going when the limit ran out, in which case, it says, it may never halt.

    A row of cells

    A cellular automaton is a row or grid of cells that all change at once, generation after generation, each by looking only at its neighbours. In a 1983 paper in Reviews of Modern Physics, Stephen Wolfram studied the simplest kind, in which each cell is alive or dead and looks at itself and its two neighbours. Those three cells can stand in eight arrangements, and a rule says which of the eight leave a live cell behind, so there are 256 rules; Wolfram numbered them 0 to 255 by reading the eight answers as a binary number. Rule 90 grows a single cell into Sierpiński’s triangle of triangles. Rule 30 grows it into a pattern that looks random, so convincingly that its centre column was later used to make random numbers. Rule 110 was shown by Matthew Cook, in a proof published in 2004, to be capable of universal computation: given the right starting row, it can do anything a Turing machine can.

    Rule 30 runs a row of 63 cells for 31 generations, starting from a single live cell in the middle, and prints one generation to a line with an asterisk for every live cell and a count of the living at the end. Cells beyond the edges are counted as dead. Watch the left edge settle into regular diagonal stripes while the right side churns without ever settling. The last generation is punched in the same form as the row card, so it can replace that card and carry the run on down another sheet of paper.

    What to try: change 30 to 90 on the first data card for the triangles, or to 110 with the single cell moved to column 63, the last, so that the pattern has room to grow leftward.

    Life on a grid

    John Conway’s Game of Life is a two-dimensional relative. It reached the public in Martin Gardner’s “Mathematical Games” column in Scientific American in October 1970. Each cell has eight neighbours. A live cell with two or three live neighbours survives; a dead cell with exactly three comes alive; every other cell is dead in the next generation. Among the first patterns found was the glider, five cells that reassemble themselves one cell further on, diagonally, every four generations. Conway offered a prize of $50 for a pattern that grows without limit, and within weeks a group at MIT led by Bill Gosper had found a glider gun that fires a new glider every 30 generations, for ever.

    Life starts with three of the classic shapes on a grid of 12 rows by 20 columns: a glider in the top left corner, a blinker that flips between a row and a column of three, and a block of four that never changes. It prints every generation with its population, twelve live cells to begin with. The grid’s edges wrap around, so the glider that crawls off the bottom or the right comes back in at the top or the left. After the eight generations on the data card the glider has moved two cells down and two to the right, and the last generation is punched as twelve row cards in the same form as the data, ready to replace them and carry the run on.

    04 Iteration

    Loops and returns

    Do the same thing again, and again. Some rules fall to 1, some fade to silence, some wear a mountain flat, and some come round to where they have been before.

    A tail and a loop, like the Greek letter rho.

    A rule nobody can prove

    Some of the simplest rules are still not understood. Lothar Collatz posed this one in 1937, two years after his doctorate: take a whole number; if it is even, halve it; if it is odd, treble it and add one; repeat. Every number anyone has tried comes down to 1 in the end, and the computers have tried a great many: as of January 2025, every starting number below 2⁷¹ has been checked. But nobody has proved that every number must. The problem has travelled under many names (the 3x+1 problem, the Syracuse problem, and the names of Ulam, Kakutani and Hasse among them), which is itself a record of how many mathematicians it has passed through.

    What makes it hard is that the path of a number is so unruly. 26 reaches 1 in ten steps. 27, its neighbour, takes 111 steps and climbs as high as 9232 before it falls.

    The 3x+1 problem follows a number all the way down, prints each value with a bar of ink beside it, and punches every value on a card. The bars grow with the logarithm of the value, so that each doubling adds the same length and the climb and the fall can both be seen on one page; a number of 9232 and a number of 2 would otherwise not fit on the same sheet. The heading tells you the number of steps and the peak. Try 97, which takes 118 steps and peaks at the same 9232, or 871, which takes 178 steps, the longest journey of any number under a thousand.

    Things that wear away

    The three decks that follow are about things that return a little less each time.

    In Ovid’s Metamorphoses the nymph Echo can only repeat the last words she hears; rejected by Narcissus, she wastes away until only her bones and her voice remain, and her bones are turned to stone. An echo in an empty room calls once, IS ANYONE THERE, and lets the call come back fourteen times, each return quieter than the one before by the ratio on the data card, 0.75. For each return it prints the loudness, the same loudness in decibels (each return is 2.5 decibels down), and a level from 0 to 9, and it punches the level. As the level falls, so does what was said: IS ANY NE THER, then I AN N TH, then only N and H, then a lone N, then a single full stop, then nothing at all from the eleventh return on. Raise the ratio toward 1 for a long, live room; lower it for a dead one.

    In 1969, in the Electronic Music Studio at Brandeis University, Alvin Lucier recorded himself reading a short text that begins “I am sitting in a room different from the one you are in now.” He played the tape back into the room and recorded it again, and again, until the resonant frequencies of the room had reinforced themselves and the words were gone, leaving only their rhythm and the sound of the room. Lucier had a stammer, and the text ends by calling the process a way to smooth out any irregularities his speech might have. The piece was first performed at the Guggenheim Museum in New York in 1970.

    I am sitting in a room plays a row of 40 random levels into a room whose walls blur each point into its neighbours, six reflections to a take, then records it again at full volume, stretched to fill the levels 0 to 9. Each take is printed and punched as 40 digits. Jagged at first, the takes lose their sharp detail first, because the blurring flattens quick changes much faster than slow ones, until all that is left is a single smooth swell, the longest wave the room can hold. The deck stops when one take sounds exactly like the last; with the seed 1969 that happens after take 20, and it prints ONLY THE ROOM IS LEFT.

    Erosion wears a ridge down. Sixty columns of stone, each up to nine levels high, stand in a rough ridge that peaks in the middle. Each year every column may lose a level, by chance; a column that stands higher than a neighbour, or at the end of the ridge, is exposed on that side and wears much faster, so peaks are cut down and edges retreat. Every five years the ridge is printed as a row of heights with the total stone that is left, and punched. With the data card as it stands the ridge starts with 408 levels of stone and after 75 years has only 8 left, a couple of worn stumps; give it more rows, and the deck will report the year in which nothing is left standing. Geologists in the age of William Morris Davis spoke of landscapes as young, mature and old; the printout shows all three on one sheet.

    Things that come back

    The corridor walks from room to room in a building where every room has a single door out, and the door always leads to the same next room: from room r you come to room r² + c, divided by the number of rooms, keeping the remainder. Because there are only so many rooms, the walk must sooner or later come back to one it has seen, and from then on it goes round the same loop for ever. Drawn on paper, the path is a tail leading into a loop, like the Greek letter rho.

    With 997 rooms, c of 7 and a start in room 1, the walk passes through 27 rooms of tail and then a loop of 19, and on the 47th step it finds itself in room 383 again and prints YOU HAVE BEEN HERE BEFORE. That is about as soon as chance would predict. As with the birthday problem, in which 23 people are enough to make a shared birthday more likely than not, a walk among N rooms usually starts repeating after something like the square root of N steps, not after N.

    In 1975 John Pollard turned that observation into a way of factoring large numbers. Walk the corridor with the number to be factored as the number of rooms. Unseen inside it, the same walk is also going round the far smaller corridor belonging to each of its prime factors, and closes its loop there much sooner; comparing the walk with itself at two speeds, the tortoise and the hare, exposes the moment and the factor with it. Richard Brent and Pollard used a refinement of the method in 1980 to split the eighth Fermat number, a number of 78 digits, into its two prime factors.

    Change the number of rooms, the constant or the first room, and every walk is a new building. Every room on the walk is punched, so the stacker holds the whole route.

    13 The catalogue

    Thirteen decks to punch

    Every deck in this issue, in the order the articles discuss them. Each one runs as it stands; its comment cards say what its data cards hold, and what it punches lands in the stacker.

    1. Studies in perception

      A picture 64 characters wide and 32 lines deep, a disc and a target of rings on a diagonal wash, printed in full stops and asterisks by ordered dithering through a 4-by-4 screen. Step back from the printout to see the greys; change the ring width or the screen on the data cards.

    2. Tzara’s hat

      Sixteen words cut from a news item, shaken in a bag and copied out in the order they come, with stanzas broken by chance. Each word drawn is punched, so the cuttings pile up in the stacker; a new seed makes a new poem.

    3. Erratum musical

      Twenty-five notes, from the F below middle C to high F, drawn from a hat once for each of three voices, as Duchamp and his sisters did in Rouen in 1913. Prints each voice by note name and punches every draw.

    4. 3 standard stoppages

      Three threads a metre long, dropped as twenty 5-centimetre pieces that turn by chance, and the new metre that each curve makes. Change the largest turn to curl or straighten them.

    5. Euclid, read aloud

      Finds the greatest common divisor of 1071 and 462, printing each of its own instructions as it carries it out. It also punches a program that gets the same answer without making a single decision: run the stacker to hear it.

    6. A busy beaver

      A three-state Turing machine that halts after 14 steps with six 1s, printing its tape and head at every step. The comment cards give Brady’s four-state champion, which runs for 107 steps.

    7. Rule 30

      A row of 63 cells that grows from a single cell into a pattern that looks random. The last row is punched to carry the run on; try Rule 90 for triangles or Rule 110.

    8. Life

      A glider, a blinker and a block on a grid of 12 by 20 cells whose edges wrap around. Watch the glider crawl one cell diagonally every four generations; the last generation is punched as new data.

    9. The 3x+1 problem

      Halve the even, treble the odd and add one: 27 takes 111 steps and climbs to 9232 before it falls to 1, drawn as a logarithmic bar chart. Try 97 or 871.

    10. An echo in an empty room

      A call returns fourteen times, 2.5 decibels quieter each time, losing letters as it fades into silence. The ratio on the data card sets how live the room is.

    11. I am sitting in a room

      Forty random levels blurred by the room and re-recorded, take after take, until only a single smooth swell, the room itself, is left and one take sounds the same as the last.

    12. Erosion

      A ridge of sixty columns of stone worn down a level at a time, fastest where it stands exposed, printed every five years with the stone that is left.

    13. The corridor

      A walk from room to room by the rule r² + c that must fall into a loop: here 27 rooms of tail and a loop of 19, the shape of Pollard’s rho.
