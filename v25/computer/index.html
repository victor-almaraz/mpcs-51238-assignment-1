    A FORTRAN workspace

    This desktop is a working model of a programmer’s workspace. Write a FORTRAN program in the Editor, or open one from the Programs folder, then run it; what it printed, what it wrote to its output, its listing and its job log all wait in the Results folder. One of the programs generates the number sieves that the composer Iannis Xenakis used to write music, and seven more write music of their own, which the Player can play.

    Eighty Columns is a course of ten sheets in the punched cards on which programs like these were once written: reading the holes, punching them, and running decks through a reader, a printer and a punch. The Calculator works in FORTRAN’s single precision and punches its display on a card, and Crible is a game of rebuilding sieves. Two folders of miscellanea hold pictures and toys: one after Xenakis, and one after the Dadaists, who made art by chance in Zürich and Paris more than forty years before he made music with a computer.

    The About folder holds nine short documents, to be read in any order: the history of FORTRAN, how a job is laid out, how to use this desktop, the language, the line printer, Xenakis and his sieves, the sieve generator, the music programs and the Player. The picture behind the icons is a night at Persepolis, in Iran, on 26 August 1971, when Xenakis’s Polytope de Persépolis filled the ruins with sound from loudspeakers and with light from fires, searchlights and lasers; it is a picture made for this desk, not a photograph of the event.

    A short history of FORTRAN

    About, document 1 of 9

    FORTRAN, short for formula translation, was created at IBM by a team led by John Backus. Its first compiler was delivered in 1957 for the IBM 704, and it let scientists and engineers write their calculations as formulas instead of as machine instructions. Few people at the time believed that a compiler could produce code as fast as a skilled programmer’s, and much of the team’s effort went into making sure that it did. The language spread quickly to other machines. FORTRAN IV followed in 1962, and in 1966 FORTRAN became one of the first programming languages with a national standard, the American one now called FORTRAN 66. This workspace runs a subset of that language.

    Programs of that era were not typed onto a screen. Each line of a program was punched onto its own 80-column card on a keypunch such as the IBM 029, which IBM introduced in 1964, and a program was a stack of cards held together with a rubber band. The deck was handed in at the computing centre’s counter, and the printout came back hours later, or the next day. Dropping a deck was a real hazard, which is why programmers punched sequence numbers into the last eight columns: a card sorter could put a scrambled deck back in order. Some also drew a diagonal stripe across the top of the deck with a felt pen, so that a card out of place showed at a glance. Eighty Columns, on the desktop, puts those cards back in your hands.

    FORTRAN gives each group of columns on a line its own job, a layout fixed by the 80-column card, and the ruler in the Editor marks the groups so that you can see them.

    ColumnsWhat they hold
    1A C or an asterisk in column 1 turns the whole line into a comment.
    1–5These columns hold an optional statement label, which is an unsigned number that other statements can refer to.
    6Any character other than a blank or a zero marks the line as a continuation of the previous statement.
    7–72The statement itself goes here.
    73–80The compiler ignores these columns. The Editor’s status line warns you when a program line runs into them, and so does the job log.

    FORTRAN ignores blanks outside of text, so G O T O 20 means the same thing as GOTO 20, and DO 10 I = 1, 5 could be punched without a single space. The keypunches and printers of the time had no lowercase letters, so FORTRAN had none either, and the workspace reads every line in capitals, whatever you type.

    The job

    About, document 2 of 9

    At a computing centre, a job was everything handed in to be run at one time: the program deck, followed by its data cards, wrapped in a few control cards that told the operating system who was paying and what to do. Here a job is simply the text in the Editor, read one line at a time, each line standing for one card. The program comes first and finishes with an END line, and every line after it is data for the program to read. Blank lines at the end of the text are left out of the job.

    A READ takes the next data line each time it runs, so a program that reads three numbers with three READ statements uses up three data lines, in order. If the program tries to read past the last data line, the job stops with the message END OF FILE ON UNIT 5, much as it would have on an IBM machine, where the card reader was unit 5. A short job looks like this:

    C     ADD TWO NUMBERS
      READ (5,10) I, J
   10 FORMAT (2I5)
      K = I + J
      WRITE (6,20) K
   20 FORMAT (1X,I6)
      STOP
      END
   12   30

    The program reads one data line, the last line of the job, as two integers of five columns each: 12 from columns 1 to 5 and 30 from columns 6 to 10. It prints 42, after a blank that the line printer takes as an instruction to move the paper up one line (see The line printer).

    A line holds at most 80 columns, as a card did. Anything past column 80 is cut off when the job runs, and the status line in the Editor tells you when a line is that long.

    Using the workspace

    About, document 3 of 9

    The icons on the desktop open the parts of the workspace, each in a window of its own. Double-click an icon, or move to it with the Tab or arrow keys and press Enter, to open it. Click a window to bring it to the front, drag it by its striped title bar, click the box in its top right corner to make it fill the screen, and click its close box in the top left corner or press Escape to close it. The menus in the menu bar hold the same commands as the buttons in the windows: File for opening, saving and running texts, Job for the results of the last run, View for the order of the icons, and Window to bring any window to the front. Clean Up Windows, in the Special menu, puts every open window back in its first place and size.

    Writing a program

    The Editor holds one text at a time. Open a program from the Programs folder, or choose Open Program from the File menu, and it replaces the text in the Editor; choose New to start an empty one. The ruler above the text marks the label field, the continuation column, the statement field and the ignored columns, and a dotted line runs down the text at column 72. The status line below the text shows the line and column of the cursor and the field it is in, and it warns you when a line runs past column 72 or past column 80. In a data line, which comes after END, all 80 columns count, so the warning comes only past column 80.

    The Tab key moves the cursor to column 7, where statements begin, or on to the next field, and Shift + Tab moves it back. Because Tab works inside the text, press Escape to leave the text; after that, Tab moves between the controls again, and a second Escape closes the window. Control + Enter, or Command + Enter on a Mac, runs the job.

    Running it

    Press Run in the Editor, or choose Run from the File menu or Run the Editor’s Text from the Job menu. The Printout window opens with whatever the program printed, and a dashed rule labelled new page marks each place where the printer moved on to a new sheet of its fan-fold paper. The Job Log, with any errors and warnings and the code of the STOP, and the Listing of the program as the interpreter read it each have a window of their own. Those three and the Output are kept in the Results folder, and they are written afresh at each run. If the job stops with errors, an alert says so and the job log opens.

    Whatever the program writes with WRITE (7,f) goes to its Output, one record to a line; it stands for the deck of cards that a card punch would have produced. From its window you can open the records in the Editor, either in place of the text or after it as data for another run, so that one program’s output can be fed to the next, as decks were carried from the punch back to the reader. You can also copy the records, download them as a text file, or play them in the Player.

    The course

    Eighty Columns is a course in punched cards, ten sheets from easy to hard, each in a window of its own. A card is a grid of 12 rows by 80 columns: click a position to punch it, or move with the arrow keys and press Space. From sheet 4 on, typing on a card punches a whole character, as a keypunch did. The interpreter that runs the Editor’s jobs also checks every answer, and Open in the Editor takes a sheet’s deck to the Editor, one card to a line, to run and change freely. The Punch Codes chart lists the holes of every character, and it is worth keeping open beside the first sheets.

    Folders and saved texts

    The disk holds everything on the desktop, and its folders can grow while the page is open. New Folder in the File menu makes a folder in the folder at the front, or on the desktop; type its name and press Enter. Save As keeps the Editor’s text as a document in a folder you choose, and Save (Control + S, or Command + S on a Mac) writes it back there. Drag an icon onto a folder, an open folder’s window, the desktop or the Trash to move it, or select it and choose Move To or Move to Trash. Rename renames a folder or a text you made. The workspace’s own documents, programs and folders are locked: they can be moved, but not renamed or thrown away.

    Putting icons in order

    The View menu orders the icons of the folder whose window is at the front, or of the desktop if no folder window is: by Name, by Kind (folders, then applications, documents, pictures, programs and texts), or by Hand, in an order of your own. To place an icon by hand, drag it between two others, onto an icon’s name or onto an icon that is not a folder, and it goes before the icon after the pointer; a blue bar shows where. Dropped on a folder’s picture, it still goes into the folder. With the keyboard, select an icon and press Option or Alt with an arrow key, or choose Move Icon Earlier or Move Icon Later from the View menu. Moving an icon by hand in a sorted folder keeps the sorted order and goes on from there by hand, and Restore Original Order puts the workspace’s own order back. In the Programs folder each group keeps its own order.

    The Calculator and Crible

    The Calculator takes the digits and + - * / from the keyboard, ^ for a power (FORTRAN’s **), and Enter or = for the result; Backspace takes back a digit, Delete clears the entry, and Escape clears everything before it closes the window. It works in single precision, as a FORTRAN REAL does, so a result keeps about seven significant digits. It shows a result in E notation if you ask, as FORMAT (E14.7) would print it, fills the display with asterisks when a result is too large for a REAL or comes from a division by zero, just as FORMAT fills a field that a number does not fit, and punches its display on the card below it. In Crible, choose a modulus and press a number of your sieve, with the mouse or with the arrow keys and Enter, to add or remove the class of that modulus through it, until your sieve is the target; the keys 2 to 9 choose the modulus, and either sieve can be played as a rhythm.

    Nothing is kept

    The disk is a model held in memory, and nothing on it is written anywhere else, so everything on it, saved texts and new folders included, is gone once the page is closed or reloaded. To keep a program, choose Download from the File menu, which saves the text in the Editor as a file on your own computer. When you open another program or start a new one, an edited text is moved to the Trash, and Put Back in the Trash window brings it back until you empty the Trash. Revert returns the text to the way it was when it was opened or last saved.

    The language

    About, document 4 of 9

    The workspace understands a single main program; subroutines and functions of your own are not supported. A name has up to six letters and digits and must start with a letter. Unless you declare otherwise, names that begin with the letters I through N hold integers, and all other names hold real numbers, a rule that let programmers use I, J and K as counters, as mathematicians use them for subscripts, without declaring anything. Programmers used to joke that in FORTRAN, God is real, unless declared integer.

    Integers are 32 bits wide, and integer division throws away the remainder, so 7/2 is 3 and 7/2*2 is 6. Real numbers have single precision, about seven significant digits, so 7.0/2.0 is 3.5. Standard FORTRAN 66 did not let integers and reals meet in one expression, except as a real raised to an integer power. The workspace is more forgiving and works such an expression out in reals, but the old habit of converting by hand still makes a program clearer: FLOAT(I) turns an integer into a real, and INT(X) drops the fraction of a real. A real assigned to an integer name is cut off the same way, so N = 2.9 sets N to 2.

    StatementWhat it does
    V = exprWorks out the expression and stores the result in V, which can be a variable or an array element. Expressions use + - * / and ** for powers, with parentheses for grouping; powers are done first, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.
    INTEGER, REALDeclare names as integers or reals, overriding the first-letter rule.
    DIMENSION A(n), B(m,n)Declares arrays of one or two dimensions, numbered from 1.
    GO TO nJumps to the statement labelled n. The computed form GO TO (n1,n2,...), I jumps to the Ith label in the list.
    IF (e) n1, n2, n3The arithmetic IF, the oldest form, from the first FORTRAN: jumps to n1, n2 or n3 when e is negative, zero or positive.
    IF (cond) stmtThe logical IF: runs stmt only when the condition is true. Conditions compare values with .LT. .LE. .EQ. .NE. .GT. .GE. and combine them with .AND. .OR. .NOT., spelt out in letters because the character sets of the first FORTRAN machines had no < or >.
    DO n I = m1, m2, m3Repeats the statements through label n while I counts from m1 to m2 in steps of m3, or in steps of 1 if m3 is left out. As in FORTRAN 66, the loop always runs at least once.
    CONTINUEDoes nothing. It usually marks the end of a DO loop.
    READ (5,f) list or READ f, listReads the next data line, laid out as FORMAT statement f describes. By the usual convention of IBM installations, unit 5 is the card reader.
    WRITE (6,f) list or PRINT f, listPrints a line on the line printer, unit 6.
    WRITE (7,f) listWrites a record to the program’s output, which stands for the card punch, unit 7. The Player can play it.
    FORMAT (...)Describes the layout of a data line, an output record or a printed line. Iw is an integer w columns wide, Fw.d and Ew.d are real numbers with d digits after the point, nX skips n columns, nHtext and 'text' are literal text, and a slash starts a new line or record. Descriptors can be repeated, as in 3I5, or grouped, as in 2(I3,F6.2).
    STOP, PAUSE, ENDSTOP ends the job, PAUSE writes a note in the job log and carries on, and END marks the last line of the program.

    The built-in functions are SQRT, ABS, IABS, FLOAT, INT, IFIX, MOD, AMOD, SIN, COS, ATAN, EXP, ALOG, ALOG10, MAX0, MIN0, AMAX1, AMIN1, and SIGN. Their names follow the first-letter rule too: IABS and MAX0 give integers, while AMOD, ALOG and AMAX1 begin with an A so that they give reals.

    Two habits of the time are worth knowing. Statement labels need not be in order, and are often numbered in tens, so that a statement can later be put between two others without renumbering. And a FORMAT statement can stand anywhere in the program, since it is never executed; many programmers gathered them all at the end, just before END.

    The line printer

    About, document 5 of 9

    Results came back on a line printer such as the IBM 1403, introduced in 1959 with the IBM 1401, which printed a whole line at a time onto continuous fan-fold paper, up to 132 characters across. A chain carrying several copies of the character set spun horizontally past the paper, behind an inked ribbon, and behind the paper stood a row of hammers, one for each print position. Each hammer struck the moment its character went by, so a line was printed in one pass of the chain. The first model printed 600 lines a minute, and the noise of a 1403 at work filled a machine room.

    The first character of each printed line is never printed. Instead, it tells the printer how far to move the paper before printing the line. This is the carriage control character:

    First characterWhat the printer does
    blankMoves the paper up one line: ordinary single spacing.
    0Leaves one blank line first.
    -Leaves two blank lines first.
    1Skips to the top of a new page.
    +Does not move the paper, so the line prints over the one before. Programmers used it to underline a heading or to darken a character by striking it twice.

    On the 1403, the skip to a new page was steered by a loop of punched paper tape in the printer’s carriage, which turned with the paper and carried a hole at the line where each page began. That is why a FORMAT for a printed line usually begins with 1X or 1H , to put a blank in the first position, and why a report begins with 1H1. Forgetting it caused famous mistakes. If a FORMAT happens to put a 1 in the first position, the printer throws a new page, so printing 123 with I3 starts a new page and prints only 23; a program that printed a column of such numbers could run through a box of paper with a single number on each sheet.

    Iannis Xenakis and the sieve

    About, document 6 of 9

    Iannis Xenakis (1922–2001) was a Greek-French composer, architect and engineer, born in Brăila, in Romania, to a Greek family. He studied civil engineering at the Athens Polytechnic, fought in the Greek resistance during the Second World War, and in the fighting in Athens in 1944–45 was badly wounded in the face and lost the sight of one eye. He came to Paris as a political refugee in 1947. He then spent twelve years in Le Corbusier’s studio, where he worked on the monastery of Sainte-Marie de La Tourette and took charge of designing the Philips Pavilion for the 1958 Brussels World’s Fair, whose curved shell of concrete was a cousin of the string glissandi of his Metastaseis. He brought an engineer’s habits of mind to music.

    Xenakis composed with probability and set theory, and he was one of the first composers to write music with a computer. In 1962 he ran his Free Stochastic Music program on an IBM 7090 at IBM France in Paris. Xenakis designed the program, and M. F. Génuys and M. J. Barraud coded it in FORTRAN IV. Given the shape of a piece, the program chose every note’s time, instrument, pitch, glissando, length and loudness by the laws of chance, and printed them as columns of numbers that Xenakis then transcribed into a score by hand. Its output became the ST pieces, among them ST/10 and ST/4, whose titles record the date the data was generated: 8 February 1962. He published the program several times, most completely in his book Formalized Music (1971), an expanded English edition of Musiques formelles (1963).

    The sieve

    During a residency in West Berlin in 1963–64, on a grant from the Ford Foundation, Xenakis conceived his theory of sieves, a way of building scales and rhythms out of simple arithmetic. A sieve is made from residual classes. The class written here as m@s (Xenakis wrote m with a subscript s) contains every integer x for which x - s is divisible by m: the numbers that leave s over when divided by m. So 3@0 is the sequence …, 0, 3, 6, 9, …, and 4@1 is …, 1, 5, 9, 13, ….

    Classes are combined by union (every number in either), intersection (only the numbers in both) and complement (every number not in the class). The union 3@0 | 4@0, counted from 0 to 12, gives 0, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12: steps of 3, 1, 2, 2, 1 and 3, an uneven rhythm that repeats exactly every 12, the least common multiple of the two moduli. The intersection of 2@0 and 3@0 is simply 6@0. With larger moduli the period grows quickly, so a sieve can sound irregular for a long time and still be wholly determined. Read the numbers as semitones and a sieve is a scale; read them as pulses and it is a rhythm.

    Xenakis first used sieves in Akrata (1964–65), for sixteen winds, and in Nomos Alpha (1965–66), for solo cello, they select the pitches from a scale in quarter tones. They became one of the mainstays of his music; in his later works, from the 1980s on, scales built from sieves underlie much of the harmony.

    The sieve generator

    About, document 7 of 9

    The fifth sample program in the Programs folder, Sieve generator (after Xenakis), is written in the same FORTRAN that the workspace runs, in the spirit of Xenakis’s own programs. Like most programs of its day, it is told what to do by its data rather than by questions on a screen. The first data line after END holds three numbers in 5-column fields. MODE is 1 for the union of the classes, 2 for their intersection, or 3 for the complement of their union. N is the number of classes, from 1 to 10, and LIMIT sets the range, because the program tests every integer from 0 to LIMIT - 1. Each of the next N lines describes one class with a modulus M and a shift S, again in 5-column fields.

    For each integer the program counts how many of the classes contain it, using MOD, and the mode decides from that count whether the integer belongs: at least one class for a union, every class for an intersection, none for a complement. It prints each member of the sieve along with its distance from the member before it, the interval that a composer would hear, and it writes each member to its output as a record of its own, so that you can feed the members to another program as data.

    The sample data describes the sieve 5@2 | 5@3 over the integers 0 to 19, which contains 2, 3, 7, 8, 12, 13, 17 and 18, with intervals alternating 1 and 4. Change the data lines at the bottom of the program and run it again to try other sieves. For example, a first line of 2, 2 and 30 (mode 2, two classes, 0 to 29), followed by classes 2 0 and 3 0, gives the intersection 0, 6, 12, 18, 24; with mode 3 and the same two classes, the program gives the numbers that are neither even nor multiples of 3, 1, 5, 7, 11 and so on. Each number goes in its own 5-column field, right-justified. Play the output in the Player to hear a sieve as a rhythm or a scale.

    The music programs

    About, document 8 of 9

    The last seven programs in the Programs folder are music programs. Each one is a FORTRAN program that writes a record to its output for every note, in the layout that the Player reads, and each one follows an idea of Xenakis’s. None of them is a reconstruction of his own programs or scores: each takes one principle and lets you hear it. Their data lines come after the END line, and the comment lines at the top of each program explain what the numbers mean, so you can change them and run the program again. Those that use chance take a seed among their data: the same seed always gives the same music, and a new seed gives a new piece built on the same rules.

    Sieve scale (a melody)
    This program takes the union of several residual classes over the semitones above a base note. The result is a scale that need not repeat at the octave, as most scales do, and a walk by chance on that scale, up to two steps up or down at a time, makes a melody.
    Two rhythm sieves
    Two sieves over a cycle of pulses strike a low drum and a high one, and the stroke is louder where they fall together. Xenakis built the rhythms of Psappha (1975), for solo percussion, from sieves; Sylvio Gualda gave its first performance in London in 1976.
    Stochastic cloud (after Achorripsis)
    Notes arrive at random at a fixed mean rate, in a band of pitch that rises over the cloud, and the printout sets the counts of notes in each cell of time against the Poisson law, which predicts how often rare, independent events arrive in a given span. In Achorripsis (1956–57), for 21 instruments, Xenakis used the Poisson law with a mean of λ = 0.6 events to a cell.
    Markov melody (after Analogique)
    A table of chances decides which of five notes follows each note, which makes the melody a Markov chain: what comes next depends only on what sounds now, not on anything before it. Xenakis used Markov chains in Analogique A (1958) and Analogique B (1959) to decide which of his clouds of sound followed which.
    String glissandi (after Metastaseis)
    Each line slides in semitone steps from a note in a cluster to a note in a wide chord, and all the lines take the same time. In bars 309–314 of Metastaseis, Xenakis drew the string glissandi as straight lines on graph paper before he wrote them out as notes.
    Brownian melody (after Mikka)
    A random walk moves the melody up or down by a few semitones at each pulse and bounces it off the edges of a range, and a note is held for as long as the walk stays on it. Mikka (1971), for solo violin, is one continuous glissando whose path comes from a random walk, the motion that physicists call Brownian, after the botanist Robert Brown, who in 1827 watched tiny particles from pollen grains jitter in water.
    Arborescence (after Evryali)
    A line climbs or falls by one or two semitones at each pulse, bends now and then, and at random splits in two, until as many as six lines sound at once. In Evryali (1973), for piano, Xenakis used arborescences, lines that branch and proliferate like a tree, so dense in places that the score needs four or five staves.

    The Player

    About, document 9 of 9

    The Player plays a program’s output as sound. A note record holds four numbers in 5-column fields, as FORMAT (4I5) writes them. TIME is the onset in pulses, counted from 0. NOTE is a MIDI note number from 0 to 127, where 60 is middle C. LEN is the length in pulses, and a blank means 1. LOUD is the loudness from 1 to 9, and a blank means 7. A pulse is a quarter of a beat, so at the starting tempo of 120 beats a minute a pulse lasts an eighth of a second.

    A record with only its first five columns filled, such as a record from the sieve generator, holds a bare number. With the Rhythm setting, the Player plays each bare number as a time, so a sieve sounds as a rhythm on middle C. With the Scale setting, it plays the numbers one per pulse in the order of the file, as semitones above the C an octave below middle C, so a sieve sounds as a scale. The setting matters only for bare numbers. The music programs write full note records that set their own times and notes, so for their files the setting is turned off. Records that are not notes, such as lines of text, are skipped, and the status line says how many were skipped.

    For example, the record    16   67    4    9 starts at pulse 16, two seconds in at the starting tempo, and plays the G above middle C for four pulses, half a second, as loud as the Player goes.

    Press Play in Player in the Output window, or Load Output in the Player, and then Play. Pause holds the place, and Stop rewinds to the start. The roll shows every note as a bar, in the manner of a player piano’s roll, with pitch running up and time across; a dotted line marks every C, and a tick under the roll marks every beat. The bars are coloured from sky blue for the quietest notes to terracotta for the loudest, and a terracotta line moves across the roll as the music plays; click the roll, or use the arrow keys on it, to move the place. The tempo control sets the beats per minute, from 40 to 240, and Double speed plays the music twice as fast and an octave higher, as a tape machine does when its speed is doubled. Sound starts only when you press Play.

    Every note sounds in the wave shown on the Player’s Wave pad. Tape, its own voice, is a triangle wave with a quiet tone an octave above it; Sine, Triangle, Reed and Saw sound in those shapes alone; and one cycle drawn on the pad with the pointer sounds as it is drawn. A new wave takes effect from the next note. The tape player on the desk has the same pad.

    This plays each vowel of these lines as a note, one syllable to a pulse, from u, the lowest, up through o, a and e to i, the highest, as the vowels run from dark to bright: a toy for this album, not a reading of the poem.

    The opening of Hugo Ball’s Karawane (1916), one of his Verse ohne Worte, poems without words. Ball and Emmy Hennings opened the Cabaret Voltaire in Zürich in February 1916, in the back room of a tavern in the Spiegelgasse, and there on 23 June 1916 Ball recited his first sound poems, in the cardboard costume, reading from music stands set up on the stage. Among them was the poem he called Elefantenkarawane, the caravan of elephants: its words belong to no language, yet its heavy, repeated syllables (blago bung, blago bung) can be heard plodding. Ball meant such poems as a renunciation of a language that, he wrote, journalism had abused and corrupted. Karawane was printed in Richard Huelsenbeck’s Dada Almanach (Berlin, 1920) with its lines set in many different typefaces; here the screen’s two faces, inverted, ruled and spaced, stand in for them. The poem goes on for eight more lines.

    To make a Dadaist poem

    recipe, with a hatTristan Tzara, 1920

    Prenez un journal.
    Prenez des ciseaux.
    Choisissez dans ce journal un article ayant la longueur que vous comptez donner à votre poème.
    Découpez l’article.

    Take a newspaper. Take some scissors. Choose an article as long as you mean your poem to be, and cut it out. Then cut out each of its words with care and put them in a bag. Shake it gently. Take the cuttings out one after another, and copy them out conscientiously in the order in which they left the bag. The poem will be like you. And there you are, an infinitely original writer, with a charming sensibility, even if the common herd does not understand it.

    The recipe from Tristan Tzara’s Dada manifeste sur l’amour faible et l’amour amer (Dada manifesto on feeble love and bitter love), which he read at the Galerie Povolozky in Paris on 12 December 1920, during an exhibition of Francis Picabia’s work; it was printed in 1921 and collected in his Sept manifestes Dada (1924). The French is its first four lines; the English, the whole recipe, is translated for this album. Tzara, a Romanian poet who had come to Zürich in 1915 and was among the Cabaret Voltaire’s first performers, offered it half as a joke, but the method outlived the joke: in 1959 the painter Brion Gysin and the writer William S. Burroughs took up the cut-up again, in a Paris hotel, with sliced-up newspapers.

        Eighty Columns
        
        
        Ten exercises in punching FORTRAN on cards
        
      
        
    
        
    
        A punched card holds one line of a program, written as holes in 80 columns and 12 rows. These ten sheets teach you to read the holes, punch them, lay out a FORTRAN statement on a card, and run decks through a card reader, a line printer and a card punch. The workspace’s own interpreter checks every answer.
        
    
        Punched cards are older than computers. Herman Hollerith’s cards and tabulating machines counted the United States census of 1890, and the company that grew from his business became IBM. By the 1960s a card of 7⅜ by 3¼ inches, with one corner cut so that a card turned the wrong way showed at once, was the ordinary way of putting a program, or almost anything else, into a computer.

        The first three sheets teach the holes themselves: how to read a card, and how to punch digits, letters and signs with the codes of the IBM 029 keypunch, which IBM introduced in 1964. The next sheets teach the parts of a FORTRAN card, continuation cards, the order of a deck, data cards, the line printer and the card punch. On the last sheet you punch a whole program of your own. The swatch beside each sheet marks its difficulty, from pale sand to dark walnut.

Every sheet has hints, and the later sheets can show a correct card if you are stuck. The sheets run from easy to hard, but they can be opened in any order, and a solved sheet offers the next one.

        The card
        
      
        IBM introduced its 80-column card in 1928, with narrow rectangular holes so that more columns fitted across the card than the round holes of earlier cards allowed. A card has 80 columns and 12 rows, and each column holds one character as a pattern of holes. From top to bottom the rows are 12, 11, 0, and then 1 to 9. A digit is a single hole in its own row. A letter is a zone hole in row 12, 11 or 0 together with one digit hole. Most signs add a hole in row 8 to a digit hole, and some add a zone hole as well. This is the Hollerith code, named after the inventor of the punched-card tabulator. Both tables below are built from the interpreter’s own code table.
        
      
        For example, A is 12 and 1, J is 11 and 1, S is 0 and 2, and the sign = is 8 and 6. Reading a column means finding its zone first, if it has one, and then its digit. In this code no character takes more than three holes.
        
    
        
    
        
      
        Single holes, and a zone hole with one digit hole
        
      
        
      
        A hole in row 8 with a digit hole from 2 to 7, and an optional zone hole
        
    
        
    
        
      
        Using a card
        
      
        Click a position on a card to punch a hole there, and click it again to fill the hole in. A real hole could not be filled in, but here it can.
        
      
        With the keyboard, press Tab to move into the card. The arrow keys move between positions, Home and End go to the first and the last column, and Page Up and Page Down go to the top and the bottom row. Space or Enter punches or clears the hole under the cursor. A screen reader announces the row, the column and whether the position is punched.
        
      
        From sheet 4 on, the keypunch is switched on. Typing a character on the card punches that character’s code into the current column and moves on to the next one, as a keypunch did. Delete clears the current column, Backspace clears the column before it, and the right arrow key leaves a column blank. Those sheets also have a keypunch line above the card, where you can type the whole card as text.

        Read a card
        
      
        This card was punched without printing, so nothing is written along its top edge. Read the holes column by column and type the text that the card holds. FORTRAN ignores blanks, and so does this check, so you need not count the empty columns.

Cards punched by other machines could come out with no printing at all, and an interpreter such as the IBM 557 read their holes and printed the characters for people to read.

              This program sets I to 7, jumps over the card that would reset it, and prints I at statement 30. Two of its cards are blank. Punch card 1 as a comment that says what the program does. Because the operator could not guess what was meant, programmers learned to write a slashed zero to tell it from the letter O, a practice whose local conventions differed from one centre to the next. The band under each card here is ruled the same way as such a form.

                Continuation cards
                
      
                The line that this program prints is too long for the 66 statement columns of one card. Card 3 holds the start of its FORMAT statement and ends with a comma. Punch card 4 as a continuation card that finishes the statement, so that the printer prints exactly the target line.
                
      
                A statement continues onto the next card when that card has any character other than a blank or a zero in column 6. Columns 1 to 5 of a continuation card stay blank, and its columns 7 to 72 are joined onto the end of the statement. Blanks outside quoted text are ignored, so the empty columns at the end of card 3 do no harm. Inside quotes, every blank is printed, and a quoted text that runs past column 72 carries on from column 7 of the next card. Programmers often punched the continuation column with the digits 1, 2, 3 and so on, so that the order of the cards was plain; FORTRAN 66 allowed up to nineteen continuation cards for one statement.

                  The dropped deck
                  
      
                  Someone dropped this deck. Every card still carries its sequence number in columns 73 to 80, so the order can be restored. Put the cards back in order, either with the card sorter or by moving them one at a time, and then run the deck to check it.
                  
      
                  The sorter reads one column of every card and drops each card into the pocket for the digit punched there. Cards with no digit in that column go to the reject pocket. The pockets are then picked up in order, from 0 to 9 with the rejects last, and the cards inside each pocket keep the order in which they arrived.
                  
      
                  On the machine. IBM’s 083 sorter read one column per pass at 1,000 cards a minute and had 13 pockets, one for each row and one for blanks and rejects. A key several columns wide took one pass per column, and operators began with the rightmost column and worked to the left: because each pass keeps the order the cards arrived in, the deck comes out sorted on the whole key after the last pass.

                    Data cards
                    
      
                    This program reads a count from its first data card, then reads that many numbers, one to a card, and prints the count and the sum. Punch data cards after the END card so that the printer prints the target line. Any numbers that give the target will do.
                    
      
                    Every card after END is data. FORMAT (I5) reads an integer from columns 1 to 5 of a card and ignores the rest of it. A blank between two digits of a field is read as a zero, so a field punched 1 2 is read as 102. Programmers were taught to punch each number hard against the right of its field, because many compilers of the time read trailing blanks as zeros too. If the program asks for a card after the last one, the job stops with END OF FILE ON UNIT 5.

                        Punch, then feed back
                        
      
                        Program A reads a number N from its data card. It punches a card holding N, and then one card for each square 1, 4, 9 and so on, up to N squared. Program B reads cards like these and prints their total. Choose N, punch it on program A’s data card, run program A, and feed the cards it punches to program B, so that program B prints the target line.
                        
      
                        The cards a program punches land in the output stacker. They are ordinary cards, so they can be carried back to the reader as data for the next job. A program cannot read the cards it punches during the same run. Many installations had a single machine that both read and punched, such as the IBM 2540 of the System/360, and long calculations were often run in stages, each stage leaving its results on cards for the next.
