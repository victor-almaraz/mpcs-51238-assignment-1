# EDITORS
title: From the editors

A black-and-white photograph is made of very little: grains of silver in a layer of gelatin, black where the light fell and clear where it did not. Everything else, every shade of grey from the shadow under a tree to the white of a cloud, is a matter of how much, and the whole craft of the photographer between the 1920s and the 1970s was the craft of how much. How much light, for how long. How much development. How much more light on this corner of the print than on that. And, at the end, how much ink on the page.

This issue is about the people who turned those amounts into rules. An engineer at Leitz who doubled a frame of cinema film and made a camera small enough for a pocket. Two friends in a chemical works in Lancashire who measured the blackness of their plates and drew it as a curve. A pianist turned photographer and a portrait photographer from Hollywood who divided the grey scale into eleven zones, so that a picture could be planned before the shutter was pressed. A young Frenchman trained as a painter, with a Leica, who thought a photograph was a matter of a fraction of a second. And the printers and engravers, and in the end the programmers, who found that a page with only one ink could still carry a photograph, if the ink was broken into dots.

Our argument is that black-and-white photography was always half arithmetic. The rules were never the art, but the art could not be had without them, and the photographers who mattered most knew the rules best and leaned on them hardest.

Five of those rules are here to be run. Wherever an article discusses a deck, a button beside the text puts it on the coding form. The line printer is a fitting darkroom, because like a printing press it has only black and paper: a full stop for paper and an asterisk for ink. With them the decks print a table of exposures, a film’s characteristic curve under three developments, the eleven zones as a printed wedge, a photogram made by chance, and a picture broken into halftone dots. The comment cards at the top of each deck say what its data cards hold. Change them, and the darkroom does as it is told.

Printed in black alone on coated paper, the plates through a screen of 150 lines to the inch.

# ARTICLE 01
kicker: The 35 mm camera, 1913–1952
title: The decisive moment
standfirst: An asthmatic engineer wanted a camera light enough to take on his travels. He turned a strip of cinema film on its side, and gave a generation of photographers a frame of 24 by 36 millimetres and a way of seeing.
pullquote: “It became the extension of my eye.”

## Two cine frames

Oskar Barnack came to the Ernst Leitz works at Wetzlar, the microscope makers, on 1 January 1911, from Carl Zeiss in Jena, to head its design department. He suffered from asthma, and he wanted a lighter camera to take on his travels than those of the day, with their glass plates, plate holders and tripods. Between 1913 and 1914 he built a small metal camera of his own that took the perforated 35 mm film made for the cinema.

The cinema ran that film vertically through the camera, and each frame was 18 by 24 millimetres. Barnack ran it sideways and let each picture take the length of two cine frames, 24 by 36 millimetres, with sides in the proportion of two to three. It is the frame that photographers still call full frame. A small negative meant a small camera, and a small negative could be enlarged later in the darkroom; the camera could be carried everywhere, and a single load of film gave thirty-six pictures.

It was ten years before Leitz made it. In 1923 Barnack persuaded his chief, Ernst Leitz II, to make a trial batch of 31; in 1924 the camera was named Leica, from the first letters of Leitz Camera; and in 1925 the Leica I was shown at the Leipzig Spring Fair. Its lens was a 50 mm of f/3.5, sold at first as the Elmax, for Ernst Leitz and the optical designer Max Berek, and renamed Elmar in October of that year when another maker objected to the name. Barnack died in 1936, by which time the small camera had changed the press, the street and the way photographs looked.

## A rule for the sun

A camera that fits in a pocket asks to be used quickly, and early Leicas had no exposure meter. Photographers carried tables, or slide rules, or a rule of thumb. The best known is still taught: in bright sun, set the lens to f/16 and the shutter to one over the film speed. A light overcast is a stop less light, so open to f/11; overcast f/8, heavy overcast f/5.6, open shade f/4; on sand or snow close to f/22.

The rule works because exposure is a product. Each stop on the lens halves or doubles the light that passes; each step on the shutter dial, 1/60 to 1/125 to 1/250, halves or doubles the time. Open one and shorten the other, and the film receives the same light. In the 1950s the German shutter maker Friedrich Deckel gave the sum a single number, the light value in Europe and the exposure value, or EV, in America: EV 0 is f/1 for one second, each step is a stop, and a bright sunny scene for film of ASA 100 comes out at EV 15.

*Sunny sixteen* does the arithmetic. As the data card stands, ASA 100 in bright sun, the rule asks for f/16 at 1/100 second, a speed that falls between the halvings of the dial; the deck works it out as EV 14.64 and calls it 15. Then it prints every stop from f/1.4 to f/32 against every speed from one second to 1/1000, and the same number runs down the table on a diagonal: that is reciprocity, set out in figures. It ends by listing the six settings that give the rule’s exposure, from f/5.6 at 1/1000 to f/32 at 1/30, and reminds the photographer that a wide stop gives less depth of field and a short time stops more motion. Try fast film in open shade, ASA 400 and f/4, and the deck finds EV 13 and eight ways to make it.

## The extension of an eye

Henri Cartier-Bresson, born in 1908, studied painting under André Lhote in 1926 and took his first photographs about the same time. In 1932 he bought his first Leica and travelled in Europe with his friends Leonor Fini and André Pieyre de Mandiargues; in 1933 he had his first exhibition, at the Julien Levy Gallery in New York. “I had just discovered the Leica,” he wrote later. “It became the extension of my eye, and I have never been separated from it since I found it.” In 1947 he founded the agency Magnum Photos with Robert Capa, David Seymour, William Vandivert and George Rodger, and from 1948 to 1950 he spent three years in Asia: in India at the death of Gandhi, in China at the change of government, in Indonesia at independence.

Then came the book. *Images à la sauvette*, initiated by the publisher Tériade, appeared from his Éditions Verve in October 1952, with a cover cut in paper by Henri Matisse and in an American edition from Simon and Schuster. The French title, worked out with Cartier-Bresson’s brother-in-law Georges Sadoul, evokes the street pedlar selling *à la sauvette*, without a licence, ready to run. Richard Simon wanted something punchier for America, and chose *The Decisive Moment*, from an epigraph Tériade had suggested, from the memoirs of the Cardinal de Retz, first published in 1717: “There is nothing in this world which does not have its decisive moment.” Matisse wrote the English title by hand at the foot of his cut-out.

The book let the 24 × 36 frame spread out across very large pages, printed in heliogravure by the Draeger brothers. Robert Capa called it “a Bible for photographers”. In its introduction Cartier-Bresson defined the thing in a sentence: “To me, photography is the simultaneous recognition, in a fraction of a second, of the significance of an event as well as of a precise organization of forms which give that event its proper expression.” The fraction of a second is the shutter’s; the recognition had to be the photographer’s, and a camera small and quiet enough to be forgotten was what made it possible.

## A curve from Widnes

Ferdinand Hurter, born in Switzerland in 1844, had studied chemistry under Robert Bunsen and physics under Gustav Kirchhoff before he settled in England as chief chemist of the Gaskell-Deacon works at Widnes, in Lancashire. Vero Charles Driffield, four years younger, came to the works in 1871. They became friends over music, and around 1876 Driffield persuaded Hurter to take up photography as a hobby. Before long the two men were measuring their plates.

They exposed plates to carefully graded amounts of light, developed them, and measured how much light each patch of the developed plate would let through. Their key step was to take a logarithm. The opacity of a patch is how many times it cuts down the light; its density is the logarithm of that opacity, so that a density of 0.3 lets through half the light and a density of 1.0 a tenth. Plotted against the logarithm of the exposure, density makes a curve that every photographer would come to know. It rises slowly from the faint fog of the unexposed plate in a curved toe, climbs a long straight line whose slope came to be called gamma, and bends over at the top in a shoulder, where more light hardly adds any more black. They published it in the *Journal of the Society of Chemical Industry* in May 1890, as “Photo-Chemical Investigations and a New Method of Determination of the Sensitiveness of Photographic Plates”. Two years before, they had built an actinograph, a slide rule for exposures.

*The characteristic curve* draws the curve three times, for three development times, as a field of dots with the curve in asterisks. The film is a model, but behaves as film does: longer development steepens the straight line, by less with each extra minute. With the times on the data card, 6.5, 8 and 10.5 minutes, gamma rises from 0.56 to 0.63 to 0.73. Along the foot of each plot the deck marks the zones (of which more below), one stop or 0.3 of log exposure apart, and tells which zone of exposure reaches the density that normal development gives Zone VIII. Under the short development it is Zone IX; under the long, Zone VII.

## Eleven zones

Ansel Adams had trained as a concert pianist and only gave up music for photography in 1930, after Paul Strand showed him a box of negatives in Taos. Fred Archer, born in 1889, was a portrait photographer who had made his name with pictures of Hollywood stars, and from 1935 taught photography at the Art Center School in Los Angeles. When the two taught together there, around 1939 and 1940, they worked out what Adams later described as not an invention of his but “a codification of the principles of sensitometry”: the Zone System.

It divides the tones of a print into eleven zones, from 0, full black, to X, the white of the paper. Each zone is one stop of exposure, twice the light of the zone below. Zone V is middle grey: clear north sky, average weathered wood. The key is the meter. A reflected-light meter knows nothing of the subject; whatever it is pointed at, it recommends the exposure that would render that surface as Zone V. So the photographer reads a surface, decides where it should fall, and gives a stop less exposure for each zone below V. The other surfaces then fall where their readings put them, a zone for every stop. Development then moves the bright tones, as the curve showed: N+1, a longer development, carries a Zone VII surface up to VIII; N−1, a shorter one, brings Zone IX down to VIII. Adams set it all out in *The Negative* in 1948.

*The Zone System* prints the eleven zones as a wedge through a screen, each a tenth more paper than the last, with the relative exposure under each, from 1 to 1,024. Then it photographs a landscape of six surfaces whose meter readings are on the data card. As it stands, the shadow under the pines reads EV 8 and is placed on Zone III, so the exposure is EV 10, f/4 at 1/60. Dark rock falls on V, foliage on VI, weathered wood on VII, granite in sun on VIII and a white cloud on IX, each marked under its zone, and the deck advises a contraction: develop N−1. Place the shadow on Zone II instead and everything falls a zone lower, the cloud lands on VIII, and the advice is to develop normally.

## Moonrise

The system’s most famous test came at speed. On 1 November 1941, by the astronomers’ later reckoning at 4:49 in the afternoon, Adams was at the village of Hernandez, in New Mexico, when he saw the moon rising over it, with the last sun on the white crosses of its graveyard. In the best-known telling of the story he could not find his meter, but he knew the luminance of the moon, 250 candles per square foot, placed it on Zone VII, and worked out the exposure from that. As he pulled the dark slide for a second negative, the sunlight left the crosses.

*Moonrise, Hernandez, New Mexico* was first published in *U.S. Camera Annual 1943*, chosen by Edward Steichen. The negative was hard to print. Adams made more than 1,300 prints from it, heightening the contrast over the years, so that later skies are far darker than early ones.

## In the darkroom

That is the other half of the craft. Adams liked to compare the negative to a composer’s score and the print to its performance. The 35 mm negative was meant to be enlarged: an enlarger is a camera in reverse, a lamp shining down through the negative and a lens that throws its image onto a sheet of paper on the baseboard. First a whole roll is laid on one sheet under glass and printed in contact, thirty-six small frames, and the photographer marks the frame worth enlarging. At the enlarger, a hand or a card on a wire held in the light for part of the exposure dodges a shadow, so that it prints lighter; a card with a hole burns in a sky. No two prints from the same negative need be quite alike.

## Shadows on paper

The first photographs had no lens. In the 1830s William Henry Fox Talbot laid leaves, flowers and lace on paper made sensitive with salt and silver nitrate and put it in the sun; where the object lay, the paper stayed pale, and everywhere else it darkened. He called the results photogenic drawings. They are negatives, white shapes on a dark ground, and they are exactly the size of what made them.

The modern artists rediscovered the method as a way of escaping the camera. By his own account in his autobiography of 1963, Man Ray found it by accident in his Paris darkroom, when an unexposed sheet of paper slipped into the developing tray and he laid a glass funnel and a thermometer on it and turned on the light. He called the results rayographs, and in 1922 put twelve of them into a portfolio, *Les Champs délicieux*, in an edition of 40, with a preface by Tristan Tzara. In the summer of 1922 László Moholy-Nagy and Lucia Moholy, his wife and a photographer, began laying objects of different shapes, textures and transparencies on sensitive paper in Berlin. It was in *Malerei, Photographie, Film*, the eighth of the Bauhaus books, in 1925, that Moholy-Nagy used the word that has stuck, photogram; the book carried only his name, though Lucia Moholy had shared the work.

*A rayograph by chance* drops things on a sheet of paper 72 characters wide and 36 lines deep, chosen and placed by chance from a seed on the data card: a button, a washer, a pencil, a loop of thread, or a glass, which lets half the light through and prints grey. Then it switches on the light. Where the light falls the paper prints black, an asterisk; where a thing lies it stays white, a full stop; so the picture comes out as Talbot’s did, white shapes on black. With the seed 1927 the paper catches a pencil, two washers, a button, two loops of thread and a glass, and the deck lists where each landed before it prints the sheet. A new seed is a new rayograph, and the same seed the same one.

## A screen of crossed lines

A press prints ink or no ink. To print a photograph with all its greys, it has to be broken into dots: big dots that merge in the shadows, small ones in the light tones, too fine for the eye to see one by one. Talbot himself proposed photographic screens in a patent of 1852. On 4 March 1880 the New York *Daily Graphic* printed “A Scene in Shantytown”, the first photograph reproduced with a full range of tones in a newspaper, made by Stephen Horgan with a crude screen. Georg Meisenbach patented a halftone process in Germany in 1882, and in Philadelphia Frederic Ives worked out a commercial method of his own. The screen that lasted came from two brothers there, Max and Louis Levy, patented in 1893: two sheets of glass ruled with fine opaque lines, cemented face to face with the lines at right angles. The photograph was copied through it onto a printing plate, and every square of the screen made one dot.

For a single ink the screen is turned to 45 degrees, where the rows of dots catch the eye least. Its fineness is counted in lines to the inch: about 85 for a newspaper, 133 to 150 for a magazine like this one.

*A halftone screen* shows how the dots work, at the coarseness of a line printer. Its picture is a ball lit from the upper left, sitting on a floor before a grey wall, with its shadow; below it, a scale of ten tones from 10 to 100 per cent. Every character is inked where the picture is darker than the screen’s threshold, which rises and falls across the page like an egg box, turned by the angle on the data card. As it stands, the pitch is five characters and the angle 45 degrees, and since the printer sets ten characters to the inch, the deck reports a screen of 2.0 lines to the inch. Watch the scale: small black dots at 10 per cent, a chequerboard at 50, small white holes near 90. Set the angle to 0 and the dots line up in rows and columns; set the pitch to 3 and the characters are too coarse to hold the dots, so that every tone from 60 per cent up fills in solid black.

## Magazines of pictures

With the halftone, photographs could be printed with the type, and by the 1930s whole magazines were built on them. *Life* published its first issue on 23 November 1936, with Margaret Bourke-White’s photograph of the Fort Peck Dam in Montana on the cover. In Britain *Picture Post* followed on 1 October 1938, founded by the publisher Edward Hulton and the Hungarian-born editor Stefan Lorant, who had developed the story told in photographs at picture weeklies in Berlin and Munich. A photographer’s work now reached most of its public not as a print at all but as a pattern of dots.

## Pictures made of characters

A computer can make that pattern itself. At Bell Labs in 1966 or 1967 Kenneth Knowlton and Leon Harmon scanned a photograph of the dancer Deborah Hay, reduced it to a grid of greys, and printed each cell as a small symbol whose density of ink matched its grey. *Studies in Perception I* was about twelve feet wide; it hung at the press conference in Robert Rauschenberg’s loft that launched Experiments in Art and Technology on 10 October 1967, and was in *The New York Times* the next day. In November 1973 Harmon showed in *Scientific American*, in “The Recognition of Faces”, how few squares of grey it takes to recognise Abraham Lincoln, with a portrait so coarse it looks like a mosaic. Three years later Salvador Dalí painted it into a picture of his own.

A line printer is the plainest of these machines: one size of mark, in one ink, on paper. Every deck in this issue is a black-and-white photograph made the hard way.
