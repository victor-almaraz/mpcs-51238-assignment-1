    FORTRAN and the punched card

    FORTRAN, short for formula translation, was created at IBM by a team led by John Backus. Its first compiler shipped in 1957 for the IBM 704, and it let scientists and engineers write their calculations as formulas instead of as machine instructions. The language spread quickly. FORTRAN IV followed in 1962, and its 1966 standard, FORTRAN 66, made FORTRAN one of the first programming languages with an official standard. This simulator runs a subset of that language.

    Programs of that era were not typed onto a screen. Each line of a program was punched onto its own 80-column card on a keypunch such as the IBM 029, which IBM introduced in 1964, and a program was a stack of cards held together with a rubber band. Dropping a deck was a real hazard. That is why programmers punched sequence numbers into the last eight columns: a card sorter could put a scrambled deck back in order.

    FORTRAN gives each group of columns on a card its own job, and the coding form shades the groups so that you can see them.

    ColumnsWhat they hold
    1A C or an asterisk in column 1 turns the whole card into a comment.
    1–5These columns hold an optional statement label, which is an unsigned number that other statements can refer to.
    6Any character other than a blank or a zero marks the card as a continuation of the previous statement.
    7–72The statement itself goes here.
    73–80The compiler ignores these columns, which traditionally held sequence numbers. The simulator warns you if anything is punched in them.

    FORTRAN ignores blanks outside of text, so G O T O 20 means the same thing as GOTO 20. The coding form accepts only the characters that the 029 keypunch could punch, and it turns lowercase letters into capitals, because the keypunch had no lowercase.

    Volume 1 · Chapter 2

    The deck

    A job is a single deck of cards. The program comes first and finishes with an END card, and every card after the END card is data for the program to read. If the program tries to read past the last data card, the job stops with the message END OF FILE ON UNIT 5, just as it would have on an IBM machine. Blank cards at the end of the deck are not sent to the reader.

    Volume 1 · Chapter 3

    Using the simulator

    The workspace is a room. This manual stands on the bookshelf in three volumes, beside the magazine in its ledge, the Xenakis miscellanea in a file box and the deck box; on the desk below are the coding form, the out tray, the computer and the tape player. Choose anything in the room to take it up, or choose it from the Menu in the top corner of the page. Whatever can be taken up is outlined in white under the pointer, with a caption that says what pressing it does; the arrow keys move from one thing to the nearest in that direction, Enter takes it up, and H outlines everything in the room that can be taken up. The prints, the plants, the lamp and the mug are outlined too: pressing one puts another of its kind in its place, and pressing the bare wall (or Change the wallpaper in the menu) hangs another paper. The switch on the wall by the lamp turns the lamp off and on. To look at something instead, press it with the right button (or press L with it under the pointer or focused): a line along the foot of the room says what it is, and each print offers the deck it calls to mind. The room has its own small sounds, which Room sounds in the menu turns off. The room is furnished afresh by chance each time the page is opened. Pressing Escape, except while typing on the coding form, puts the object down and returns to the room. In the manual, the contents list and the Previous page and Next page buttons turn the chapters, and so do the left and right arrow keys.

    The deck box holds every sample deck, each behind a tab with its name: the programs, the music decks, and the decks printed in the magazine, which the magazine’s articles can also put on the form. Picking up a deck puts it on the coding form in place of the cards that were there. On the coding form you can also type your own cards. Pressing Enter adds a new card below the current one, pressing Backspace on an empty card removes it, and the up and down arrow keys move between cards. If you paste several lines at once, each line becomes a card of its own. When the deck is ready, press Run deck.

    Under the form lies the card in hand: the card selected on the form, with its holes. The Previous card and Next card buttons step through the deck, and the form’s selection follows along.

    After each run, the out tray comes to the front. It holds the printer output, the job log with any errors and warnings, a listing of the program, and the output stacker, where any cards that the program punched land. From the stacker you can load them onto the coding form as data for another run, copy them, download them as a text file, thread them onto the tape player, or read them into the computer.

    The computer on the desk has a desktop of its own, with an Editor in place of the coding form, files in place of decks, a Player, a course in punched cards, a calculator, a game and two folders of miscellanea, and its own Read Me. The card punch and the computer share their programs. Type into the computer, under the coding form, opens the deck in the computer’s Editor as a text, one line to a card, and Read into the computer, at the stacker, opens the punched cards there. In the computer, Punch on Cards in the File menu sends the Editor’s text back to the coding form, one card to a line. Back to the Room, in the computer’s first menu, or the Back to the room button in the corner, leaves the computer as it is; it keeps its windows and files until the page is reloaded.

    The deck is kept only while the page is open, so reloading the page brings back the first sample.

    Volume 1 · Chapter 4

    The language

    The simulator understands a single main program; subroutines and functions are not supported. A name has up to six letters and digits and must start with a letter. Unless you declare otherwise, names that begin with the letters I through N hold integers, and all other names hold real numbers. Integers are 32 bits wide, and integer division throws away the remainder, so 7/2 is 3. Real numbers have single precision.

    StatementWhat it does
    V = exprThis evaluates the expression and stores the result in V, which can be a variable or an array element. Expressions use + - * / and ** for powers, with parentheses for grouping.
    INTEGER, REALThese declare names as integers or reals, overriding the first-letter rule.
    DIMENSION A(n), B(m,n)This declares arrays of one or two dimensions, numbered from 1.
    GO TO nThis jumps to the statement labeled n. The computed form GO TO (n1,n2,...), I jumps to the Ith label in the list.
    IF (e) n1, n2, n3This jumps to n1, n2, or n3 when e is negative, zero, or positive.
    IF (cond) stmtThis runs stmt only when the condition is true. Conditions compare values with .LT. .LE. .EQ. .NE. .GT. .GE. and combine them with .AND. .OR. .NOT.
    DO n I = m1, m2, m3This repeats the statements through label n while I counts from m1 to m2 in steps of m3, or in steps of 1 if m3 is left out. As in FORTRAN 66, the loop always runs at least once.
    CONTINUEThis does nothing. It usually marks the end of a DO loop.
    READ (5,f) list or READ f, listThis reads the next data card, laid out as FORMAT statement f describes.
    WRITE (6,f) list or PRINT f, listThis prints a line on the line printer.
    PUNCH f, list or WRITE (7,f) listThis punches a new card into the output stacker.
    FORMAT (...)This describes the layout of a card or a printed line. Iw is an integer w columns wide, Fw.d and Ew.d are real numbers with d digits after the point, nX skips n columns, nHtext and 'text' are literal text, and a slash starts a new line or card. Descriptors can be repeated, as in 3I5, or grouped, as in 2(I3,F6.2).
    STOP, PAUSE, ENDSTOP ends the job, PAUSE writes a note in the job log and carries on, and END marks the last card of the program.

    The built-in functions are SQRT, ABS, IABS, FLOAT, INT, IFIX, MOD, AMOD, SIN, COS, ATAN, EXP, ALOG, ALOG10, MAX0, MIN0, AMAX1, AMIN1, and SIGN.

    Volume 1 · Chapter 5

    The line printer

    Results came back on a line printer such as the IBM 1403, which printed a whole line at a time onto continuous fan-fold paper. A chain of type spun past a row of hammers, and each hammer fired the moment its character went by. The first character of each printed line is never printed. Instead, it tells the printer how far to move the paper. A blank moves it one line, a 0 leaves a blank line first, a minus sign leaves two blank lines, a 1 jumps to the top of a new page, and a plus sign prints over the line before. This rule caused famous mistakes. If a FORMAT happens to put a 1 in the first position, the printer throws a new page, so printing 123 with I3 starts a new page and prints only 23.

    Volume 2 · Chapter 6

    Iannis Xenakis and the sieve

    Iannis Xenakis (1922–2001) was a Greek-French composer, architect, and engineer, born in Romania to a Greek family. He came to Paris as a political refugee in 1947. He then spent twelve years in Le Corbusier’s studio, where he took charge of designing the Philips Pavilion for the 1958 Brussels World’s Fair, and he brought an engineer’s habits of mind to music. He composed with probability and set theory, and he was one of the first composers to write music with a computer. In 1962 he ran his Free Stochastic Music program on an IBM 7090 at IBM France in Paris. Xenakis designed the program, and M. F. Génuys and M. J. Barraud coded it in FORTRAN IV. Its output became the ST pieces, among them ST/10 and ST/4, whose titles record the date the data was generated: 8 February 1962. He published the program several times, most completely in his book Formalized Music (1971).

    During a residency in Berlin in 1963–64, Xenakis conceived his theory of sieves, a way of building scales and rhythms out of simple arithmetic. A sieve is made from residual classes. The class written here as m@s (Xenakis wrote m with a subscript s) contains every integer x for which x − s is divisible by m, so 3@0 is the sequence …, 0, 3, 6, 9, …. Combining classes by union, intersection, and complement produces patterns that repeat exactly but sound irregular. He first used sieves in Akrata (1964–65), and in Nomos Alpha (1965–66) for solo cello they select the pitches from a microtonal scale.

    Volume 2 · Chapter 7

    The sieve generator deck

    The fifth sample deck is a sieve generator written in the same FORTRAN that the simulator runs, in the spirit of Xenakis’s own programs. The first data card after END holds three numbers in 5-column fields. MODE is 1 for the union of the classes, 2 for their intersection, or 3 for the complement of their union. N is the number of classes, from 1 to 10, and LIMIT sets the range, because the program tests every integer from 0 to LIMIT − 1. Each of the next N cards describes one class with a modulus M and a shift S, again in 5-column fields.

    The program prints each member of the sieve along with its distance from the member before it, and it punches each member onto a card of its own, so that you can feed the members to another program as data. The sample data describes the sieve 5@2 | 5@3 over the integers 0 to 19, which contains 2, 3, 7, 8, 12, 13, 17, and 18. To hear the idea in numbers rather than notes, change the three data cards at the bottom of the deck and run it again. To hear it in notes, thread the punched members onto the tape player.

    Volume 3 · Chapter 8

    The music decks

    The music decks are the second group in the deck box. Each one is a FORTRAN program that punches a card for every note, in the layout that the tape player reads, and each one follows an idea of Xenakis’s. Their data cards come after the END card, and the comment cards at the top of each deck explain what the numbers mean, so you can change them and run the deck again.

    Sieve scale (a melody)
    This deck takes the union of several residual classes over the semitones above a base note. The result is a scale that need not repeat at the octave, and a walk by chance on that scale, up to two steps up or down at a time, makes a melody.
    Two rhythm sieves
    Two sieves over a cycle of pulses strike a low drum and a high one, and the stroke is louder where they fall together. Xenakis built the rhythms of Psappha (1975), for solo percussion, from sieves.
    Stochastic cloud (after Achorripsis)
    Notes arrive at random at a fixed mean rate, in a band of pitch that rises over the cloud, and the printout sets the counts of notes in each cell of time against the Poisson law. In Achorripsis (1956–57), for 21 instruments, Xenakis used the Poisson law with λ = 0.6 to decide how many events fall in each cell of a matrix of 28 units of time by 7 timbres.
    Markov melody (after Analogique)
    A table of chances decides which of five notes follows each note, which makes the melody a Markov chain. Xenakis used Markov chains in Analogique A (1958), for nine strings, and Analogique B (1959), for sine tones on tape.
    String glissandi (after Metastaseis)
    Each line slides in semitone steps from a note in a cluster to a note in a wide chord, and all the lines take the same time. In bars 309–314 of Metastaseis, Xenakis drew the string glissandi as straight lines on a graph of pitch against time.

    Volume 3 · Chapter 9

    The tape player

    The tape player plays the cards in the output stacker as sound. A tape card holds four numbers in 5-column fields, as FORMAT (4I5) punches them. TIME is the onset in pulses, counted from 0. NOTE is a MIDI note number from 0 to 127, where 60 is middle C. LEN is the length in pulses, and a blank means 1. LOUD is the loudness from 1 to 9, and a blank means 7. A pulse is a quarter of a beat, so at the starting tempo of 120 beats a minute a pulse lasts an eighth of a second.

    A card with only its first five columns punched, such as a card from the sieve generator, holds a bare number. With the Rhythm switch, the player plays each bare number as a time, so a sieve sounds as a rhythm on middle C. With the Scale switch, it plays the numbers one per pulse in card order, as semitones above the C an octave below middle C, so a sieve sounds as a scale. The switch matters only for bare numbers: the music decks punch full tape cards that set their own times and notes, so for their tapes the switch is turned off. Cards that are not tape cards, such as cards of text, are skipped, and the player says how many it skipped.

    Press Thread onto the tape player at the stacker, or Thread tape from the stacker on the tape player, to wind the stacker’s cards onto the reels, and then press Play. Pause holds the place, and Stop rewinds the tape to the start. The tempo control sets the beats per minute. The speed switch changes the tape speed from 7½ to 15 inches per second, which, as on a real machine, plays the music twice as fast and an octave higher. The player adds a slow wobble in pitch, a softened treble, and a faint hiss, as a tape recording would have. Sound starts only when you press Play.

    From the editors

    This issue is about the early meeting of art and computing, when a picture, a poem or a score could come out of a deck of punched cards. Its four articles follow pictures made by programs, works left to chance or written as instructions, machines that do nothing but follow rules, and rules that loop and return.

    Wherever an article discusses a deck, you can put that deck on the coding form, run it, and collect what it makes from the line printer and the card punch: a picture printed in characters, a poem drawn from a hat, a machine writing on its tape, or a pattern that grows by rule. The catalogue at the back lists all thirteen.

    Every deck is written in the FORTRAN of the rest of the desk. Its data cards come after the END card, and the comment cards at the top say what the numbers mean, so you can change them and run the deck again.

    Computer graphics, 1965–1968

    Pictures from programs

    “Not one picture was sold.”

    Pictures made by computer were first exhibited in 1965. From 5 to 19 February, the Studiengalerie of the Technische Hochschule Stuttgart showed graphics by Georg Nees, generated by a digital computer, under the auspices of a colloquium led by the philosopher Max Bense. In April, the Howard Wise Gallery in New York showed Computer-Generated Pictures by Béla Julesz and A. Michael Noll of Bell Telephone Laboratories, made on an IBM 7094 with a microfilm plotter. Its invitation was a small deck of four punched cards in different colours, and not one picture was sold. In November, Nees and Frieder Nake showed their work together at the Galerie Wendelin Niedlich in Stuttgart.

    At Bell Labs, Kenneth Knowlton and Leon Harmon made Studies in Perception I, which collections date to 1966 or 1967. They reduced a photograph of the dancer and choreographer Deborah Hay to a grid of grey squares and replaced each square with a small symbol, taken from mathematics and electronics, whose density matched its grey. The print was about 12 feet long. It was reproduced in The New York Times on 11 October 1967, and the Museum of Modern Art showed it in The Machine as Seen at the End of the Mechanical Age (25 November 1968 to 9 February 1969).

    The idea is the same as a halftone, in which a picture is printed as dots whose size follows its tone, and Studies in perception carries it out with the two marks this printer has. The deck works out a tone from 0 to 16 for each cell of a picture 64 characters wide and 32 lines deep. It compares each tone with a 4-by-4 screen of thresholds read from a data card, and it inks the cell wherever the tone is higher, a method called ordered dithering. The picture is this page’s own: a black disc and a target of rings on a diagonal wash. Change the ring width or the screen on the data cards and run it again. Every row is also punched as 64 digits.

    Three years later, from 2 August to 20 October 1968, Cybernetic Serendipity at the Institute of Contemporary Arts in London, curated by Jasia Reichardt, set out to show computer-aided creative work of every kind: art, music, poetry, dance, sculpture and animation, along with painting machines and works in which chance played a large part. About 60,000 people saw it.

    Dada, Duchamp and Fluxus

    Chance and instructions

    “The idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

    Long before computers, Dada artists let chance do the composing. Tzara’s hat follows Tristan Tzara’s 1920 recipe for a Dadaist poem: sixteen words cut from a newspaper item are shaken in a bag and copied out in the order they come, and each word drawn is also punched, so the cuttings pile up in the stacker. Erratum musical repeats what Marcel Duchamp did in Rouen in 1913, when he drew 25 notes, from the F below middle C up to high F, out of a hat, once for each of three voices: his sisters Yvonne and Magdeleine, and himself. 3 standard stoppages drops three threads, each one metre long, the way Duchamp did in 1913–14, and measures the new metre that each curve makes. Every deck starts from the seed on its data card, so a different seed gives a different poem, score, or set of threads, and the same seed always gives the same one.

    In the 1960s, artists around Fluxus wrote works as instructions for someone else to carry out. La Monte Young’s Composition 1960 #10, dated October 1960 and dedicated to Robert Morris, reads in full: “Draw a straight line and follow it.” At the Fluxus festival in Wiesbaden in September 1962, Nam June Paik performed it by dipping his head in ink and drawing a line down a long sheet of paper on the floor. In “Paragraphs on Conceptual Art” (Artforum, June 1967), Sol LeWitt wrote that “the idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

    A program is an instruction piece performed by a machine, and Euclid, read aloud performs one of the oldest. It is the method for finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers in Book VII of Euclid’s Elements, written about 300 BC, which Donald Knuth called “the granddaddy of all algorithms.” The deck prints each of its own instructions as it carries it out, along with what happened. It also punches a second program: the steps it actually took, with every test already decided and the loop written out in full. Load the stacker in place of the deck and run it, and the new program prints the same answer without making a single decision.

    Computation theory

    Machines that follow rules

    “Which one leaves the most 1s behind?”

    In 1936 Alan Turing described a machine that does nothing but read and write symbols on a tape, one square at a time, following a table of instructions, and he argued that such a machine can carry out any calculation that a person following fixed rules can. His paper “On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem” was received by the London Mathematical Society on 28 May 1936 and read on 12 November 1936.

    In May 1962, Tibor Radó asked a question that sounds like a game, in “On Non-Computable Functions” in the Bell System Technical Journal. Of all the machines with a given number of states that start on a blank tape and eventually halt, which one leaves the most 1s behind? He proved that no program can answer that for every number of states. Shen Lin and Radó settled three states in 1965: the most is six 1s, and no three-state machine that halts runs for more than 21 steps. Allen Brady settled four states in 1983, with 13 ones and 107 steps. A busy beaver runs a three-state champion, which halts after 14 steps with six 1s, and prints the tape and the head at every step. The machine itself is data: its table is on the cards after END, and the deck’s comments give the four-state champion.

    A cellular automaton is a row of cells that all change at once, step after step, each by looking only at its neighbours. In a 1983 paper in Reviews of Modern Physics, Stephen Wolfram studied the simplest kind, in which each cell is 0 or 1 and looks at itself and its two neighbours, and numbered the 256 possible rules from 0 to 255. Rule 30 grows from a single cell into a pattern that looks random. Rule 110 was shown to be capable of universal computation by Matthew Cook, in a proof published in 2004. Rule 30 prints one generation to a line and punches the last one, so that the punched card can replace the row card and carry the run on. Its comments say how to try Rule 90 or Rule 110.

    John Conway’s Game of Life is a two-dimensional relative. It reached the public in Martin Gardner’s “Mathematical Games” column in Scientific American in October 1970. Each cell has eight neighbours. A live cell with two or three live neighbours survives, a dead cell with exactly three comes alive, and every other cell is dead in the next generation. Life runs a glider, which crawls one cell diagonally every four generations, beside a blinker and a block, on a grid of 12 by 20 cells whose edges wrap around.

    Iteration

    Loops and returns

    “A tail and a loop, like the Greek letter rho.”

    Some of the simplest rules are still not understood. Lothar Collatz posed this one in 1937: halve an even number, treble an odd one and add one, and repeat. Every number anyone has tried comes down to 1 in the end, but nobody has proved that every number must. The 3x+1 problem follows 27, which takes 111 steps and climbs as high as 9232 before it falls, and prints each value with a bar whose length grows with the value’s logarithm.

    The four decks that follow are about things that wear away or come back. An echo in an empty room lets a call return more quietly each time, losing letters as it fades. I am sitting in a room follows Alvin Lucier’s 1969 piece, in which he recorded his voice, played the tape back into the room, and recorded it again until only the room’s resonances were left; here a row of random levels is blurred and re-recorded until one take sounds the same as the last. Erosion wears a ridge of stone down a level at a time, faster where it stands exposed.

    The corridor walks from room to room, where every room has a single door that always leads to the same next room, given by the rule r2 + c divided by the number of rooms, keeping the remainder. Because there are only so many rooms, the walk must come back to one it has seen, and from then on it goes round the same loop for ever: a tail and a loop, like the Greek letter rho. John Pollard used the same kind of walk in his 1975 method for factoring large numbers.
